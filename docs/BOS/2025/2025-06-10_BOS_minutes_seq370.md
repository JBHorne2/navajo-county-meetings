Consideration and acceptance of the resignation of Pamela Phillips as the Public Fiduciary Director and appointment of Eileen Rogers as the new Public Fiduciary Director, effective 7/1/2025 :
Pamela Phillips expressed sincere gratitude to the board for the opportunity to serve as the Navajo County Public Fiduciary over the past three and a half years. She spoke about the honor and privilege of the role, especially given the importance of the work and the vulnerable population they serve, which are often individuals who have no one else to help them. She thanked Bryan Layton, HR, Administration, and Jayson Vowell for their support and guidance during a time of significant transition, acknowledging how much that help meant during an initially overwhelming period.  She introduced Eileen Rogers, praising her as an essential part of the team and someone without whom the job could not have been done. She stated that Ms. Rogers has worked for the county for over 30 years and over 20 years in the department, noting that her experience, knowledge, compassion, and empathy make her exceptionally qualified for the role. She recommended that the Board accept her resignation and appoint Eileen Rogers as the next Public Fiduciary.
Eileen Rogers expressed gratitude for Ms. Phillip's leadership, describing her as a great boss and role model. She requested consideration to be appointed as the next Public Fiduciary, highlighting 22 years of service in the office. She spoke of the continuous learning that comes from the job, conferences, and even clients. She indicated that, while excited to take the next step, she will miss the people she currently serves.
Vice-Chair Whitesinger expressed deep gratitude to Ms. Phillips for her 19 years of dedicated service, praising her compassion and the unique impact of her department. She spoke of the lasting legacy she leaves behind and extended her best wishes to Ms. Rogers, recognizing the special commitment required to serve in such a role.
Supervisor Whitesinger made a motion  to accept the resignation of Pamela Phillips as the Public Fiduciary Director and appoint Eileen Rogers as the new Public Fiduciary Director, effective 7/1/2025, motion seconded by  Supervisor Benally .
Chairman Whiting thanked both individuals for their service to Navajo County.  He wished Ms. Phillips well in the next chapter of her life, and acknowledged the servant's heart required for the role, expressing deep appreciation for how well she fulfilled it.
Vote unanimous approving the motion.

WORK SESSION :
The Work Session was called to order at 10:08 a.m.

a.

Budget Presentation :
Bryan Layton emphasized the importance of transparency and public engagement in public works initiatives. He indicated that the county wants to not only implement great programs based on data but also share those programs with the public in a clear, open manner. He further indicated that Madhav Mundle will share information and ways of communicating public works efforts to the communities.
Madhav Mundle provided an overview of the Capital Improvement Program (CIP) and highway maintenance. He explained that the CIP is a five-year plan covering 2026 to 2030, with funding from various grants like RAISE, E-Z Smart, and potentially an OSB grant. He spoke about highway maintenance, focusing on transparency and public information. He further spoke about a new feature on the Public Works website: gravel road maintenance reports, noting that the reports provide detailed information about road maintenance, including current and previous quarterly reports, specific details of road grading, location and length of roadways and district-specific information.  Mr. Mundle highlighted that more than 45% of their roads are gravel roads and that they try to maintain these roads every eight weeks. He indicated that the reports allow residents to easily search and find when and where their specific roads were graded. He noted that the goal is to make this information readily accessible to the public, helping residents understand the county's road maintenance efforts.
Chairman Whiting acknowledged the efforts of Mr. Mundle in making road maintenance information transparent and accessible. He shared his appreciation to the department for responding to board members' requests by providing detailed road maintenance reports, noting that these reports can help address common inquiries from residents, such as distinguishing between county and private roads. He requested clarification about the road maintenance frequency.
Mr. Mundle confirmed that unpaved roads are graded approximately every eight weeks, with current and previous quarterly reports available to help residents track maintenance activities.
Mr. Mundle discussed gravel road stabilization. He explained that over 45% of their roads are gravel roads, including forest service, primitive, and regular gravel roads. He indicated that they currently maintain roads by spraying water and using motor graders every eight weeks, often ahead of schedule. He further indicated that the county is experimenting with road stabilization techniques to reduce dust and improve road durability, noting that they are testing different admixtures and materials to find solutions that work with their varied soil conditions. He stated that their approach involves selecting one subdivision every alternate year for stabilization, conducting pilot projects in different districts, trying new materials to improve road conditions and maintaining routine maintenance alongside stabilization efforts.  He indicated that previous pilot projects have yielded mixed results, however, they continue to research and test methods to find the most effective stabilization technique for their diverse county terrain.
Mr. Layton spoke about the gravel road stabilization efforts, explaining that paving these roads would be extremely expensive, as it requires comprehensive road design, drainage considerations, and significant infrastructure changes.  He indicated that they are trying innovative, low-cost techniques to make gravel roads more passable and reduce maintenance needs. Mr. Layton noted that they are experimenting with organic materials like tree saps and other techniques proven in other areas, however, Navajo County's unique challenges include diverse soil types and varying temperatures across different districts, which means solutions that work in one area may not work in another. He indicated that the goal is to find cost-effective ways to improve road conditions without the massive expense of full paving.
Chairman Whiting reviewed the maintenance approach for gravel vs. paved roads, noting that it is his understanding that one approach is ongoing routine maintenance, while the other is a proactive effort to improve and stabilize road conditions over time.
Supervisor Benally inquired if gravel road stabilization is more than putting gravel down.
Mr. Mundle provided additional information regarding the gravel road stabilization project, indicating that the process involves more than just adding gravel, it includes adding extra admixtures and chemicals, blending them into the road, and compacting the surface. He stated that the goal is to create a more durable road that remains stable for a longer period and potentially reduces maintenance cycles. Mr. Mundle spoke about the cost-effectiveness of this approach, noting that it costs only 1-5% of what it would take to construct a brand-new roadway, which is significantly cheaper because building a new road involves extensive and expensive processes like widening rights-of-way, addressing drainage issues, adding striping and signage, and potentially replacing sub-grade materials. He noted they have selected 8-9 subdivisions, with plans to stabilize 4-5 this year and the rest next year. He indicated that these efforts are focused only on unincorporated areas, and the project aims to improve road conditions while keeping costs minimal.
Supervisor Seymore shared concerns about road maintenance, emphasizing the need to consider increased traffic flow in various areas. He indicated that some roads may require more frequent grading due to higher traffic volumes, such as Burton Road, noting how a road that was once in excellent condition has deteriorated to a washboard-like state. He recommended not just focusing on admixtures, but also potentially redoing the road base, suggesting basalt base as a good mixture that holds up well in most terrains. He indicated that they should always be looking at ways to improve and maintain roads, especially in high-traffic areas.
Mr. Mundle explained that their stabilization plan involves not just adding admixtures, but also blending and compacting the base, noting that their goal is to create a more durable road surface that can withstand heavier traffic and potential speeding. He indicated that by improving the road's base and using stabilization techniques, they hope to reduce road damage even when drivers exceed the posted 25 mph speed limit. He stated that the aim is to create a road surface that can sustain heavy traffic and remain more resilient to wear and tear.
Supervisor Benally shared her experience with road stabilization, specifically referencing a project on Highway 41. She indicated that while the initial road treatment was perfect for about two to three years, it eventually broke down noting that the treated areas returned to their original clay surface, and that she believes the method might work better for slower traffic but is less effective on a major road like Highway 41 through the Black Mesa region.
Mr. Mundle indicated that if the road stabilization method lasted for two to three years, it would be considered successful. He stated this as a positive outcome, which is why they plan to continue the stabilization project every other year, learning and improving from each attempt.
Supervisor Benally asked about road maintenance for stabilized roads, specifically inquiring if maintenance would be similar to paved roads, such as fixing potholes in the same manner, but using different materials. She inquired how much wash boarding is expected.
Mr. Mundle indicated that when a stabilized road develops potholes or wash boarding, they would send a motor grader to smooth out the surface and fix the issues, similar to their current maintenance approach for gravel roads. He further indicated that if drivers follow the speed limit, they shouldn't experience much wash boarding. He noted that on primitive roads, where they have less control over road conditions, high-speed driving can quickly cause the road surface to deteriorate, and it's best for drivers to maintain a lower speed to preserve the road's condition.  Mr. Mundle spoke about the Pavement Management Program, indicating that the county hired a consultant to collect data on road conditions using a laser-guided van to assess distress, potholes, and cracking. He stated that the consultant generated a Pavement Condition Index (PCI) for each road, and that while many agencies stop at generating this index, Navajo County went a step further and created a comprehensive 10-year Pavement Preservation Plan, which includes detailed cost estimates for road treatments, specific streets and recommended treatments, inflation-adjusted costs and a strategic approach to road maintenance.  He indicated that maintaining all roads in fair to good condition would require $4 million annually, however, the county only has $2 million available for road preservation, so they've strategically selected projects worth $2 million for the first year, focusing on slurry seal treatments and prioritizing roads based on their condition and available budget. Mr. Mundle spoke about the importance of using data-driven decision-making to manage road maintenance efficiently within budget constraints.
Chairman Whiting spoke about the need for better state-level funding for road maintenance. He emphasized that the current gas tax system, created in 1985, is no longer sufficient to maintain roads. He noted that Arizona's roads are deteriorating due to inadequate funding and suggested that the state needs to find a new funding mechanism that accounts for changes like electric vehicles, which don't contribute to the traditional gas tax.
Mr. Layton noted that while they can empathize with the funding issue, they now have clear data to demonstrate the specific funding gap in Navajo County and indicated that by presenting concrete numbers and analysis, they can speak credibly about the road maintenance shortfall and the need for additional funding. He suggested using this data-driven approach to engage with the state and potentially develop more effective funding solutions for road infrastructure.
Mr. Mundle spoke about the need for around $4 million annually to maintain roads properly. He explained that if they don't address road maintenance each year, the costs will continue to increase due to inflation and that to keep the 10-year plan manageable, they've aimed to keep the annual cost around $4 million, adjusting for inflation. He indicated that this allows them to create a reasonable long-term road maintenance strategy within their financial constraints.
Mr. Layton spoke about discussing potential priorities at the County Managers Association meeting. He suggested that if the board has a strong sentiment about road funding or infrastructure challenges, he can bring those concerns to the meeting to help staff understand what research and work they should prioritize based on board input.
Mr. Mundle discussed the fiscal year 2026 pavement preservation project. He highlighted a specific project in the Heber subdivision involving slurry seal work on High Country Pines. He indicated that while this is just one example for the current fiscal year, they plan to add more projects, noting that the long-term goal is to eventually integrate these projects into a GIS platform, allowing real-time public access to information about road maintenance. He indicated that this would include details like when and where maintenance was performed, creating a transparent and interactive way for residents to track road improvement efforts.
Rochelle Lacapa provided an overview of the transportation infrastructure funding challenges in Arizona. She indicated that the data shows that purchasing power for highway user funds has decreased by 67%, vehicle license tax has exceeded gas tax for the first time, the number of poor roads in Arizona is increasing, and ADOT estimates a $112 million shortfall for infrastructure.  She outlined potential tools available to the legislature and counties for financing road improvements, including gas and fuel tax, which requires a super majority vote; increasing vehicle license tax; creating alternate fuel tax parity; county road excise tax; regional transportation excise tax and road improvement districts.  She noted that while the Arizona County Supervisors Association has a policy resolution supporting infrastructure investment, they are not running specific bills. She suggests that Navajo County could potentially take the lead in proposing a bill through CSA, emphasizing the need for increased funding to address road maintenance challenges.
Supervisor Seymore inquired about the potential costs of putting a county road excise tax to a vote. He indicated that the state may not address the road maintenance problem soon and suggested exploring a local option where Navajo County residents could vote on a dedicated road tax. He inquired about the initial costs and feasibility of putting such a referendum on the ballot.
A discussion was held about potential funding solutions for road maintenance.  Ms. Lacapa explains the challenges of implementing a county road excise tax, noting the current jail district tax generates $6 million annually. Mr. Layton suggested the campaign costs could be several thousand dollars. Supervisor Seymore proposed exploring a county road excise tax and potentially collaborating with cities and towns to address road maintenance shortfalls. Supervisor Whitesinger indicated that she agreed with Supervisor Seymore, recognizing the ongoing challenges with state funding. She asked about the super majority requirement and potential strategies for changing voting thresholds. Ms. Lacapa explained the super majority rules and suggested that lowering the threshold might face significant push backs from tax-averse groups. Mr. Layton reviewed the board's comments about exploring state-level funding solutions, investigating local funding options, considering polling to understand public sentiment and potentially engage with local townships and communities and ask for staff to gather more information about potential funding mechanisms, explore polling to understand the public's position for a road tax, and to continue discussions about addressing road maintenance funding gaps.
A presentation was shown.  Mr. Layton discussed the budget, highlighting how the county has worked to improve employee compensation since 2016, including implementing consistent cost of living adjustments, creating longevity adjustments, redesigning pay ranges to be market-comparable, developing progression plans for career development, and establishing anniversary step increases to help employees move up pay ranges.  He spoke about how employee pay distribution has improved, with a more natural spread across pay ranges. He discussed the current budget constraints and proposed a modest cost of living adjustment, discontinuing anniversary step increases, focusing on merit-based pay, and introducing a new sabbatical program for long-term employees.  He indicated that the sabbatical program would offer 2 days off at 5 years, 1 week at 10 years and 2 weeks at 15 years, with the option to sell back time, noting that the time would have to be used consecutively within a 12-month period. He further indicated that the goal is to provide employees with an opportunity to create meaningful experiences, give them resources to potentially travel or do something special, recognize and appreciate long-term service and create an emotional connection between employees and the county.  He emphasized that this is a low-cost initiative with potentially high emotional impact and that they hope employees will remember the experience and appreciate the county's support.
Mr. Scott shared an experience about an employee who worked in the Public Works Road Yard for 30 years and their comment about how the county has significantly improved its recognition program, taking employee appreciation to a new level. He spoke about the positive impact of the county's evolving approach to recognizing and valuing long-term employees.
A discussion was held regarding the timeline for using the sabbatical time.  Mr. Layton indicated that the goal is to encourage employees to actually use their time off, rather than accumulating unused vacation, noting that employees would work with their supervisors to plan and schedule their sabbatical time within the 12-month window.
Mr. Layton discussed the county's vehicle and capital spending strategy.  He indicated as to the vehicle fleet that Sheriff's patrol vehicles are replaced at 160,000 miles and patrol vehicles cost around $70-80,000 each.  He further indicated that the goal is to replace 10 vehicles annually to maintain low mileage and that the volunteer vehicles are repurposed older patrol cars.  He spoke about the IT Infrastructure, noting that computers are replaced every four years which is a centralized replacement strategy, that the servers and network software remain expensive and that cloud migration ongoing.  He further spoke about building upgrades, including upgrading the door and camera security systems, planning bathroom remodels, and preparing to repurpose Taylor building space.  He indicated that upcoming major projects include a full roof replacement which is estimated at $5 million, an HVAC system overhaul and replacing outdated control boards and systems.  He indicated that the total capital spending is estimated at approximately $3.4 million, with funds allocated for unexpected repairs and replacements, emphasizing being frugal while preventing infrastructure deterioration.
Jayson Vowell discussed the treasurer system replacement project, which has been budgeted and delayed multiple times. He explained that some projects like the treasurer system, facility upgrades, and fire alarm system have been held over from previous years.
Mr. Layton discussed healthcare costs and the county's successful management strategy. He indicated that key points include healthcare inflation, noting that national healthcare inflation is 7% this year, which is a 22% increase over past five years, that Navajo County's inflation has been 0-2% and that employee premiums have remained flat for nine years.  He stated that successful strategies include encouraging use of Health Guide services, negotiating cash prices with providers, connecting employees to primary care, providing specialized health programs, and offering quick payment to providers.  He spoke about the
Incentives, which include $500 added to HSA for employees getting an annual physical and $300 added for the spouse's physical, noting that the goal is to encourage preventive healthcare and that the current participation is around 52% of employees.  He indicated that the approach focuses on investing in employee health early to reduce long-term healthcare costs and improve overall employee well-being.
Mr. Scott shared a story about an employee who got a physical primarily to receive the HSA incentive and that during this physical, the doctor discovered a significant medical concern that the employee believes could have saved their life. He indicated that this is not an isolated incident and highlighted the importance of the county's health incentive program in potentially catching serious health issues early.
Mr. Layton stated that the current annual physical participation rate is 52%.  He indicated that to increase participation they would propose maintaining current HSA contributions, adding a $500 one-time cash payment for employees getting physicals and an additional $300 if spouse also gets a physical, noting that they goal is to increase participation to 60-70%.
Chairman Whiting shared his appreciation for the incentive approach.
Supervisor Seymore highlighted how the HSA helps employees manage their health deductible.
Ms. Lacapa spoke about the challenge of finding primary care providers.
Mr. Layton and Mr. Scott discussed partnering with the Public Health Department to provide on-site wellness checks and potentially establish an employee clinic day, addressing the shortage of primary care providers, noting the importance of preventive healthcare and finding innovative ways to support employee health.
Mr. Scott discussed law enforcement stress management and mental health, noting that the previously implemented counseling for detectives based on the Craig Tiger Act and sending detectives to the valley for mandatory annual counseling. Mr. Layton indicated that moving forward they will have a licensed counselor, Kimberly O'Farrell, on staff to provide support and that they will expand counseling to include all deputies and animal care officers.  He spoke about the program goals which include helping employees manage emotional stress, developing strategies for handling trauma, maintaining positive relationships in and out of work and providing proactive mental health support.  He further spoke about the communication strategy which includes conducting face-to-face meetings with staff, using internal communication channels, conducting outreach to explain new programs and ensuring employees understand available support services.
Chairman Whiting emphasized the importance of proactive communication and continuous improvement in sharing information with employees.
Mr. Layton discussed recommended improvements and budget proposals, including staffing and position recommendations for a new custodial position for the Taylor building, a Recorder's office salary adjustments to align with market rates, the Treasurer's office getting a Loomis cash recycler for improved cash handling, Security Operations Center support, and two new administrator positions in the Sheriff's office.  He further spoke about the key benefits of the recommendations which include improved cash handling security, enhanced management capacity in Sheriff's office, addressing specific departmental needs and maintaining competitive salary structures.  Mr. Layton emphasized these recommendations are carefully considered to support departmental efficiency and employee needs while maintaining fiscal responsibility.
Mr. Vowell provided a budget overview, noting that FY 2025 was $45.3 million in revenues and expenditures and that FY2026 has a projected increase to $46.5 million, with a potential revenue increase to $47.7 million.  He spoke about budget adjustments, noting that a 2.5% across-the-board adjustment would cost $570,000, which includes funding for the custodial positions, salary adjustments and departmental needs.  He further spoke about funding considerations which include three primary revenue sources: property tax, county sales tax, state shared sales tax, noting that the current property tax rate is third lowest in state and the potential property tax increase could generate $218,000-$500,000.  He emphasized the need to balance budget constraints with departmental needs and focus on maintaining a fiscally responsible approach while supporting county operations.
Mr. Layton stated that they plan to propose the budget on July 15, 2025, and adopt the budget in August. He asked if the Board had feedback regarding whether they are going in the right direction and if there are any suggestions or adjustments they would like to see before the formal budget proposal.
Vice-Chair Whitesinger shared her appreciation of the budget preparation not just focusing on the current year, but also taking into consideration future implications. She commended the approach of being transparent with departments about budget constraints and emphasized the importance of protecting employees and maintaining service quality. She indicated that she values the organization's integrity and commitment to investing in employees while maintaining professional service standards.
Mr. Vowell emphasized that their fiscal planning is not just about FY2026 but about positioning the county for financial stability in future years (2027-2029), noting that the goal is to make prudent decisions now that will provide a solid financial foundation for the coming years, avoiding the need to make drastic adjustments or cutbacks later.
Supervisor Seymore thanked the team for their work on the budget. He acknowledged the potential for lean years ahead but indicated that he feels confident in the county's capacity to adjust. He shared his appreciation for the approach of valuing employees not just through pay, but through comprehensive support like healthcare and career development opportunities.
