Consideration and approval of the proposed initial Navajo County Middle Mile Fiber Pricing (Pricing Number 2025-001), the proposed Indefeasible Right of Use (IRU) agreement template, and a proposed pre-sale campaign :
Grant Evans discussed the item indicating that the presentation covers two key items for consideration: the initial pricing structure for Navajo County's middle mile broadband infrastructure and the Indefeasible Right of Use (IRU) agreement, which serves as the long-term fiber lease mechanism. He introduced Kyle Hildebrand from Ex2, the county's partner in design, construction, and marketing, who will present updates on leasing efforts and pricing. He stated the construction is progressing well and is approximately 75% complete. Mr. Evans stated that the project faced early delays during the permitting phase, which slowed the initial construction ramp-up. He stated that originally, the Arizona Commerce Authority grant deadline was June 30, 2025, but it has been extended to June 30, 2026, due to those delays. He again emphasized that construction is 75% complete, and the project is expected to finish by the end of this year. Mr. Evans stated that Ex2 has begun initial lease discussions with potential users, making it a timely moment to present the pricing structure and lease agreement to the Board to support final negotiations and marketing efforts. Kyle Hildebrand, Co-Founder and Chief Revenue Officer of Ex2, was again introduced as a key partner in the project. He oversees business development, project development, and marketing, and has been an integral and instrumental part of guiding the broadband initiative. Mr. Hildebrand indicated that they have a dedicated team focused on commercialization and monetizing Navajo County's broadband infrastructure, including experts in business, legal, and technical areas. He stated that efforts began once permitting was completed, starting with a marketing analysis to identify potential commercial users and network connection points. Negotiations were initiated with entities like APS, ADOT, NTUA, and private carriers. He stated that this led to the development of a formal commercialization and marketing plan tailored to Navajo County, prioritizing community benefits—broadband access, emergency services, economic development, healthcare, and education—over profit. The plan includes a pricing strategy for leasing dark fiber and uses Indefeasible Right of Use (IRU) agreements, offering commercial users 20-year access with certain ownership-like benefits. Mr. Hildebrand then spoke about the pricing component. He stated that the proposed pricing structure for Navajo County's broadband infrastructure includes a 20-year lease (IRU) for dark fiber only, with no lit service or conduit included. The pricing is competitive, based on market research, and designed to lower barriers to entry, encourage last-mile competition, and attract a broad range of users, including ISPs, healthcare providers, wireless companies, and energy firms. He stated that the key terms are a one-time, upfront payment, an annual maintenance fee: paid yearly, adjusted for inflation via CPI, longer lease terms may be negotiated, and pricing covers the entire route and mirrors similar models used by ADOT and APS, where Ex2 also acts as an agent. Mr. Hildebrand stated that over 50 interested entities have been engaged, and approval of the pricing is needed now to proceed with negotiations. The focus remains on enabling broadband access for residents and supporting state initiatives like the BEAD program, with marketing efforts planned to further drive interest. Grant Evans spoke about the IRU agreement, the telecommunications industry standard for executing fiber lease deals. He stated that the agreement outlines the terms and conditions of the leases and will support Ex2 in final negotiations with end users. Mr. Evans stated that staff recommended approval of both the pricing structure and the IRU agreement template. Supervisor Whitesinger raised a question about the IRU pricing structure, specifically why the IRU fee is a one-time payment rather than an annual fee. She then clarified that the question refers to the initial IRU fee (not the ongoing maintenance fee) and sought further explanation to better understand the reasoning behind this industry-standard approach. Mr. Hildebrand stated that the one-time upfront IRU fee is standard in the dark fiber and telecommunications industry because it allows carriers to capitalize the cost as an asset. While some asset owners offer annual payment options, that structure is more typical of standard lease agreements rather than traditional IRUs. Given the nature of this infrastructure, the recommended model is the one-time IRU fee plus annual maintenance. Supervisor Seymore raised a question about the potential for cost recovery on the broadband infrastructure investment. He expressed concern that, given the current pricing, there seems to be little chance of recovering the $22 million spent on the project. He cited a rough calculation—800 multiplied by 192 fibers, totaling around 153,000—as an example of the limited revenue potential under the proposed pricing structure. Mr. Hildebrand stated that its per fiber per mile, so you would take that 153,00 then times that by 105.

Supervisor Whitesinger made a motion  to approve the proposed initial Navajo County Middle Mile Fiber Pricing (Pricing Number 2025-001), the proposed Indefeasible Right of Use (IRU) agreement template, and a proposed pre-sale campaign, motion seconded by  Supervisor Seymore .  Vote unanimous approving the motion.

Presentation/Summary of Navajo County's Fiscal Year 2024 Annual Financial Report, Report on Internal Control and on Compliance, and Federal Single Audit Report :
Jayson Vowell introduced Ben Hur an auditor from the audit firm of Fester and Chapman who conducted Navajo County's audit and Stephanie Gerber from the Auditor General's Office. Mr. Vowell stated that Navajo County was the third out of 15 counties to issue financial statements, the second to issue the single audit for federal grant programs, and the first to release the expenditure limitation report, which was issued last Friday. Mr. Vowell turned the time over to Stephanie Gerber technical manager on behalf of the Auditor General. Ms. Gerber briefly explained the Auditor General's role in response to the audit. The Arizona Auditor General oversees annual audits for all Arizona counties, either conducting them directly or contracting them out due to staffing and workload. For the county's fiscal year 2024 audit, they contracted with Fester Chapman, a qualified CPA firm experienced in county audits. The Auditor General's office collaborates with the county team throughout the process and reviews the final draft reports to ensure they meet standards and accurately reflect the county's financial state and findings. Ben Hur presented the County's audit for the Fiscal Year ending June 30, 2024. Mr. Hur indicated that the presentation consists of two parts, an audit results summary which includes the financial statement audit, the single audit, and the Report to the Board of Supervisors, all of which have been completed and provided to the Board of Supervisors. The second part of the presentation is the financial overview which is a summary based on data from the audited financial statements. Mr. Hur indicated that the First Reserve Summary pertains to the financial statement audit of the County, for which a modified opinion was issued. This indicates that the financial statements are fairly presented in all material respects in accordance with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), which is considered a high-level audit opinion. However, two significant deficiencies were reported, both related to weaknesses in internal controls over IT systems. The Second Reader Summary covers the Single Audit, which is required when a non-federal agency receives over $750,000 in federal funds. Mr. Hur stated that as part of this audit, three major federal programs were selected and tested for compliance with applicable laws and regulations: the WIOA Cluster (Assistance Listing #17.258 and #17.259), the COVID-19 State and Local Fiscal Recovery Funds (Listing #21.027), and the COVID-19 Public Health Support Program (Listing #93.931). Findings from this audit included four significant deficiencies and one material weakness, all of which are detailed in the audit report submitted to the Board of Supervisors. Mr. Hur continued to report that This section represents a required communication from the auditors to the County's governing body, summarizing key aspects of the audit process. It outlines any significant events, estimates, or disclosures included in the financial statements. Mr. Hur reported no difficulties encountered during the audit and no disagreements with management regarding the audit results. As previously noted, the audit identified six significant deficiencies and one material weakness. The financial summary, based on audited financial statements as of June 30, 2024, shows the County reported total assets and deferred outflows of approximately $243 million. This includes $135 million in cash and investments, $66 million in capital assets, and $50 million in receivables from other governments. On the liability and deferred inflow side, a total of $95 million was reported, which includes $46 million in non-current pension and OPEB liabilities, $26 million in long-term debt, and $8.5 million in unearned revenue. The County's total net position was $150 million, consisting of $58 million in net investment in capital assets, $100 million in restricted net position, and an unrestricted deficit of $13 million. Mr. Hur continued to report that in fiscal year 2024, the County reported total revenues of approximately $141.6 million. This included $49 million from operating grants and contributions, accounting for about 35% of total revenues. Additionally, $20 million came from capital grants and contributions, $17 million from state-shared taxes, and $13.7 million from property taxes. On the expense side, the County reported total expenditure of $94 million. Of this, $30.9 million was allocated to general government functions, and $30 million—about 32% of total expenses—was dedicated to public safety. Other significant expenditures included $14 million for highways and streets and $10 million for health and welfare services. Chairman Whiting thanked Mr. Hur and Ms. Gerber for the presentation. Chairman Whiting further thanked Mr. Jayson Vowell and his team for all the work that they do.

Presentation regarding the current wildfire conditions and fire restrictions :
Catrina Jenkins discussed the current wildfire conditions and fire restrictions indicating that they are starting to see more activity with a new fire starting this morning. Mrs. Jenkins spoke about the White Mountain Fire Coordination Group that meets weekly to discuss and determine fire restrictions in the local region. Mrs. Jenkins indicated that the group consists of the National Forest Service partners, State Forestry partners, the BIA Fort Apache Agency Bureau of Land Management, Emergency Management from Navajo and Apache Counties, as well as a representative from the Northeastern Arizona Fire Chiefs Association as well as the Northeastern Arizona Police Chiefs Association. The group receives weekly updates and reports on the weather forecasts. Mrs. Jenkins stated that the group considers several factors, including available resources, fuel conditions, the number of fires starts, and the number of people in the region. For instance, during busy times like Memorial Day weekend, they anticipate an increase in activity. Mrs. Jenkins reported that the drought conditions have only slightly changed since last week. The region remains in severe to extreme drought, though not at the highest levels. Recent moisture, especially near I-40, slightly improved conditions, but a lack of winter snowpack remains a concern. Temperatures have been below normal, and precipitation has been above normal in late April and early May, which has influenced the fire outlook. The short-term drought is expected to continue, as it does annually. Mrs. Jenkins continued to report that the updated fire outlooks for May show improvement due to recent rain, while June still shows high fire potential. Restrictions have increased in some areas like the Tonto National Forest which is in Stage 2 restrictions. The dryness is typical, progressing from east to west, as do monsoons. July is expected to bring increased monsoonal activity. Mrs. Jenkins stated that currently, the area is in Stage 1 fire restrictions, but that may change. She emphasized that most fires this year have started in residential areas, not forests, often due to human activity, like a charcoal grill fire that destroyed a home. The Coyote Fire, which burned over 1,000 acres, started from a structure and spread rapidly. Mrs. Jenkins strongly stressed community responsibility, especially in wildland-urban interface areas, and urged locals and visitors alike to stay vigilant. Finally, Mrs. Jenkins highlighted community outreach efforts, including a successful expo in March and the upcoming Firewise Block Party, which aims to educate residents on fire safety and preparedness. Chairman Whiting thanked Mrs. Jenkins for her presentation and for consistently keeping the Board informed, both during board meetings and through emails.

Review of Renewable Energy Ordinance :
Bryan Layton explained that the meeting would include a recap of previous discussions, a report on listening sessions, a review of community concerns, a discussion on the need for renewable energy in Navajo County, and some recommendations for moving forward. He clarified that counties have limited powers compared to cities and towns and can only enact ordinances within their defined responsibilities. They cannot ban certain businesses unless they are already prohibited by law but can impose reasonable and justifiable restrictions for public benefit. He mentioned that after the last work session some of the ideas talked about were performance criteria, setbacks, and possibly a separate solar ordinance. He indicated that the Board's direction was to engage the community, consult stakeholders, research relevant processes, and explore best practices. He stated that they conducted three listening sessions, gathered online feedback, as well as received direct correspondence. Chelle Ewald reported on the listening sessions in Show Low, Heber, and Joseph City, as well as through an online survey and through direct correspondence via email and letter. She stated that there were 142 comments from 106 individuals, which staff have worked into the following critical points. Ms. Ewald spoke about private property rights, stating that we heard from landowners and ranchers that ranching is a cash-poor business, and they have worked hard to purchase land because they believe in the future of this area, which they feel is renewable energy. She further stated that we also heard from landowners who want to protect the viewshed and scenic vistas in this area indicating that the request was to respect landowners' rights to lease land for renewable energy while ensuring protection for neighbors and community interests and to avoid excessive setbacks or restrictions that unduly limit land use, but reasonable buffers should be considered to mitigate impacts on adjacent properties. Ms. Ewald then spoke about another main theme that emerged was siting and setbacks. She stated that comments on this topic were the most varied, with some people advocating for removing setbacks, while others advocated for setbacks upwards of 10 miles. From these comments, staff heard to continue to use clear, evidence-based setback requirements from residences and property lines, and consider setbacks for sensitive areas such as national forests and scenic vistas. Staff also heard that provisions should allow for flexibility and be tailored based on the project's size and location. Ms. Ewald reviewed the environmental & wildlife comments that were received indicating that many residents expressed concerns about the potential impact of wind turbines on wildlife, particularly birds and bats. However, staff also heard from a representative of the White Mountain Nature Center explaining that leaving cats outside, glass windows, and rat poison kill more birds than wind turbines, and they would like those concerns addressed first. Ms. Ewald stated that we understand from those comments that Navajo County should continue to require environmental impact assessments for all projects, mandate the use of best-available, commercially proven technology to minimize wildlife impacts, and continue to include and codify requirements for wildlife corridors, vegetative screening, and habitat preservation. Ms. Ewald reported on the decommissioning and restoration comments that were received, stating that many residents expressed concern over decommissioning practices and want to see the land returned to its natural state. She reported on solar panel manufacturers and construction companies emphasizing that the solar panel racks can be formed to the shape of the land now, and it is not like it was years ago, when the land had to be graded flat. Ms. Ewald stated that from those comments staff heard that the new Ordinance should continue to require developers to submit and fund decommissioning plans before project approval and continue to establish bonds or begin requiring cash escrow accounts and continue specifying standards for land reclamation and restoration to pre-project conditions. Ms. Ewald reported on comments on engagement and transparency which staff heard from the public about increasing the current notification requirements, continue mandating public forums and retain provisions to require outreach to affected property owners, and to continue requiring developers to document and respond to community concerns. Ms. Ewald reported on the economic and community benefits comments that were heard from local business owners that these renewable energy projects bring jobs to the local community and help put food on the table for families that live here. She stated that staff also heard from local school districts and board members that these projects support the community and bring in tax revenue. Based on that, staff understood requiring projects to contribute to local tax base, schools, and community organizations, requiring economic modeling of projected tax revenue, job creation, and community benefits, and explore mechanisms for compensating nearby residents for documented property value losses. Ms. Ewald reported on the quality of life and scenic preservation comments received, reporting that staff understood that Navajo County should continue including noise minimization standards and include standards to minimize light pollution and visual impact and also consider height restrictions for wind turbines. Ms. Ewald reported on the streamlined and predictable permitting comments received, stating that staff understood that Navajo County should continue to define clear timelines and requirements to avoid unnecessary delays while ensuring robust review and create separate ordinance for solar projects. Ms. Ewald reported on a meeting with the Fire Chiefs, thanking the Fire Chiefs and local safety personnel for their input and engagement with the process. She reported on what was learned from that meeting stating that Fire Chiefs reported unclear what their role was in the initial permit review and scope of authority, they were unclear of standards for construction, they had concern with traversing roads in fire apparatus and concern with fighting battery storage fires and they had an undefined responsibility and expectations for inspections and emergency response. Bryan Layton reported on a meeting with the local municipalities, indicating that some areas in the southern part of the county see little to no impact from turbines, as shown on upcoming maps. He stated that while the board initially considered mandatory setbacks around municipalities to protect them from turbine developments, feedback from partners suggests this may hinder economic development. Instead of rigid setbacks, partners prefer a collaborative approach where they can provide input on project proposals through consultation. Mr. Layton advised that everything that is in the Board's packet of information is also located on the County's website. Chelle Ewald thanked the stakeholders who brought new ideas and concerns to staff's attention and spoke about decommissioning and abandonment of turbines. She stated that some of the concerns raised were based on case studies regarding the following: The Palmer's Creek Wind Farm in Minnesota had a decommissioning bond of $400,000, and it is now estimated that decommissioning will cost approximately $7 million, or $400,000 per turbine. The municipality placed a moratorium on renewable energy projects and went through a similar process of updating its ordinance to improve its bonding practices. Alpha Ventus is an offshore wind farm in Germany that is nearing the end of its 20-year life expectancy. As the first offshore wind farm in the North Sea, there is some confusion about the best practices for dismantling the wind farm. However, there has been talk about how decommissioning could bring revenue to the municipalities, and there are attempts being made to repurpose the wind farm. Butera and Monte Arci are two Italian wind farms that were decommissioned shortly after construction. Research conducted into the decommissioning of wind farms in Europe and South America has found that poor planning, mismanagement of funds, and lack of oversight are the most common reasons for abandoned turbines. Ms. Ewald stated that these examples highlight the need for appropriate decommissioning plans and bonding. Ms. Ewald went on to discuss concerns with the Federal Aviation Administration, indicating that concerns about renewable projects interfering with flight paths, and residents pointed out the Lava Run Wind Farm in a neighboring county. She stated that the current ordinance mandates FAA compliance, and all projects must receive a no-hazard determination. Navajo County also coordinates with Luke Air Force Base to ensure there is no interference with the flight paths. The key takeaway is that Navajo County should and must continue complying with FAA policies and regulations. Ms. Ewald reported on citizen concerns on renewable energy projects that failed to pay their taxes. These comments likely refer to the Rock Falls Wind Farm in Oklahoma, where the company tried to claim that it was tax-exempt. That claim was dropped, and over $2 million was paid in taxes in 2018 when this issue arose. Ms. Ewald stated that what staff learned from this is that we need to continue working closely with our local Assessor, Mike Montandon, and it highlights the type of tax revenue that can be associated with these projects. Ms. Ewald went on to explain concerns raised over failed decommissioning bonds. Some case studies that were brought to our attention include Viking Energy in Scotland, where construction began without a bond in place. The bond was later received, and the project is operational today. Another case study that was highlighted was the Murra Warra Wind Farm in Australia, where there were concerns that the project faced financial collapse. However, an investor bought the project, and this one is also operational to this day. The final example, Ipswich Wind in Massachusetts, relates to a turbine that had no bond in place and caught fire. The local government is still trying to assess the best option for decommissioning moving forward. These examples emphasize the importance of having bonding in place before project construction. Ms. Ewald explained the residents' concerns over diminished property values stating that there have been several US-based, peer-reviewed studies that found that there was no significant impact on property values. The most recent study in 2023 found that property values in counties with a population over 250,000 can be impacted from the time the project is announced and lasts up to five years afterward but then go away. Ms. Ewald reported on concerns with catastrophic turbine failure stating that there is an offshore wind farm about 15 miles outside of Nantucket where a turbine blade failed due to insufficient bonding material on the blade. This incident caused the federal government to suspend operations while it investigated the fallout, as sharp fiberglass shards were washing up on the beach. The key takeaway from this incident is the importance of using the best, proven, commercially available technology to avoid these types of incidents. Ms. Ewald spoke about the final concern that stakeholders had regarding the potential for harm to wildlife. Studies have shown that wind turbines impact bird and bat populations as well as land mammals. However, work is being done by organizations like the US Geological Survey to minimize these harms, and they emphasize the importance of properly siting these projects. A professor in Scotland also notes that agricultural land is particularly suited to renewable energy projects because the land is already disturbed. Ms. Ewald stated that we should continue to require environmental impact studies and use the best available technology. Chairman Whiting asked for clarification on whether the board has any authority over wildlife issues. Mischa Larisch confirmed that while it doesn't directly oversee wildlife impacts, developers are required to conduct studies and coordinate with Arizona Game and Fish and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, who are the agencies responsible for determining any wildlife-related impacts. Bryan Layton stated that a common question is why there is specific interest in developing power generation in Navajo County rather than elsewhere in Arizona. He further stated that the answer involves several clear reasons, which will be explained using maps and input from utility representatives like APS and SRP. It's considered a valid and important question that generates significant public interest. Cody Cooper explained Navajo County's suitability for renewable energy projects by highlighting several key factors. He referenced the county's comprehensive planning area and showed maps of proposed, approved, and under-construction renewable projects. Mr. Cooper pointed out the robust transmission infrastructure in the area, noting that existing lines are looking for power to be transmitted. He discussed wind current studies and solar irradiance maps that demonstrate the county's strong renewable energy potential. He also emphasized that there are landowners willing to work with renewable energy industries, and that the county has been aware of these resources for over a decade, particularly through the Aztec area plan.  He pointed out specific projects like the West Camp Wind Farm and Dry Lake Wind Farm, explaining that project locations are strategically chosen based on wind strength and landowner cooperation. He also noted similar mapping for solar projects, indicating viable locations near Joseph City, the county center, and Sun Valley. He indicated that the projects are typically sited close to transmission lines to reduce infrastructure costs and make them more marketable.  Mr. Layton highlighted Arizona's tax incentives for renewable energy projects, noting an 80% property tax discount. He explained that the combination of tax incentives, available renewable resources, participating landowners, and existing infrastructure makes Navajo County an attractive location for these projects. He indicated that this comprehensive business case helps explain why companies are interested in developing renewable energy in the area.  Chairman Whiting expressed concern about the property tax structure, noting that due to state statute, only 20% of the project's value can be taxed. He indicated that while this is not within the county's control, the state legislature should review and potentially modify this policy to ensure that communities hosting these projects can benefit more fully from the tax revenue.  Mr. Layton explained how property tax dollars are distributed, indicating that when a property tax dollar comes into the county, over 75% goes to schools, fire districts, and other municipal services. He further indicated that a smaller portion goes to county government for basic services like law enforcement, courts, and prosecution. He emphasized that even with the limited tax revenue, there are still potential benefits to schools and local services from these renewable energy projects.  Supervisor Seymore asked about the accuracy of the evaluation numbers they received, seeking confirmation about the financial projections related to the renewable energy projects.
Mr. Layton indicated the Assessor is helping them look at the numbers so they can provide accurate information.  Janet Dean, the Northeast Public Affairs Manager for APS, discussed APS's role as a regulated public utility serving Arizona for over 140 years. She highlighted the significant changes in energy demand, noting that APS's peak demand is expected to grow from 8,200 megawatts to over 13,000 megawatts by 2038 - a 60% increase in just 14 years. She indicated that this growth is driven by population influx, business expansion, and changing customer needs. Ms. Dean explained that APS conducts comprehensive studies every three years to forecast energy needs, considering factors like economic growth, new technologies, weather predictions, and climate change. She indicated that the utilities use a competitive procurement process to determine new energy resources, ensuring a balanced and diverse energy mix that includes nuclear, solar, wind, battery, and natural gas, noting that this approach aims to maintain reliability, keep costs low, and provide resilience against supply chain disruptions and changing market conditions. She emphasized the need for a robust but adaptive strategy to meet unprecedented energy demand and transition to a cleaner, more cost-effective energy system.  Buchanan Davis, the local Government Affairs Director with SRP, highlighted SRP as a non-profit utility serving nearly 2 million customers with 5,000 employees. He emphasized that SRP is seeing significant growth, projecting they will need to double their load in the next 10 years due to population and economic expansion. He noted their commitment to an "all of the above" energy strategy, which includes existing facilities like biomass, coal, and nuclear plants, as well as renewables. Mr. Davis also addressed the misconception that these projects are being built for California, stressing that they are actually being developed to meet Arizona's growing energy needs.  Chairman Whiting asked about the potential for utilizing the existing Cholla infrastructure for future energy technologies. He inquired whether there might be opportunities to retrofit the facility for new technologies, recognizing that while no significant investments had been made recently, there seems to be renewed interest in exploring alternative uses for the site.  Ms. Dean explained that APS recognizes the value of the Cholla site for future generation, noting that while initially they thought they might decommission it, the unprecedented changes in energy technology have led them to keep the site available for potential future generation. She mentioned that APS, SRP, and TP are jointly studying locations for future nuclear power, and all coal plant sites, including Cholla, are part of this assessment. Ms. Dean noted they are in conversations with the Department of Energy about the recent federal announcement, but do not have updates to share yet. She emphasized their commitment to maintaining the site for potential future energy generation, though the specific technology remains uncertain.   Chairman Whiting indicated that he was glad to hear of the potential for using the existing infrastructure and transmission lines, noting the possibility of exploring technologies like nuclear or natural gas. He emphasized the importance of staying in conversation about these opportunities and being supportive of efforts that could benefit the local community.  Ms. Dean acknowledged the significant impact on the community, recognizing not just its economic importance but also its role in the local identity. She confirmed that APS recognizes the site's value for future generation, though the specific technology is yet to be determined.  Chairman Whiting expressed appreciation for APS and SRP's approach of being open partners and collaborating to find forward-looking solutions.  Mr. Davis shared that SRP is actively exploring converting the Coronado Generating Station to natural gas, which could extend its operational life by a decade or more, noting that they are making decisions in the next few months and have been investigating various technologies, including a pilot project for long-term storage. He indicated that SRP has announced that coal operations will cease no later than 2032, which provides time to explore alternative options.  Supervisor Seymore thanked the Ms. Dean and Mr. Davis for their partnerships and the insights that they have provided.
Supervisor Whitesinger sought clarification on the megawatt numbers presented, asking how these relate specifically to Navajo County and what the exponential increases mean in terms of energy production and local impact, noting that she wanted to understand the scale of growth and its direct implications for the county.  Ms. Dean explained that the 8,200 megawatts and 13,000 megawatts projections are for APS's entire state footprint, not specific to Navajo County. She acknowledged that Navajo County's growth has been steady, without the dramatic spikes seen in other areas like industrial developments with data centers. She noted that some communities in Navajo County have significant expansion plans with corresponding energy needs. She indicated that she will obtain more specific numbers for Navajo County.  She shared that one megawatt typically serves about 1,000 homes, with a large box store consuming approximately one megawatt.  Supervisor Seymore discussed recent legislative pressures related to energy development, including attempts to allow data centers to install small nuclear facilities without county or city oversight. He also mentioned solar companies pushing for immediate permit issuance for residential solar installations. He emphasized the importance of maintaining regulatory oversight and working with good partners who want to follow proper procedures, rather than simply bending to the demands of large companies.  Mr. Davis highlighted the interconnectedness of Arizona's economy, particularly between the White Mountains and the valley. He noted that when things are going well in the valley, it benefits the entire state through economic growth, tourism, and other opportunities. He further emphasized SRP's commitment to being a proactive partner, wanting to work with developers early to address potential concerns and minimize negative impacts of renewable energy projects.  Ms. Dean emphasized APS's commitment to balancing growth with reliability and affordability for existing customers, noting that while they want to support economic development and accommodate new industrial customers like data centers, they are focused on ensuring that these developments do not come at the expense of current customers' service quality or cost.
Mr. Layton highlighted the complexity of renewable energy development, noting the many different perspectives and stakeholders involved. He emphasized that the county's goal is to create an ordinance that is reasonable and justifiable, recognizing that different stakeholders can have varying opinions on the same issues. He indicated that the listening sessions helped confirm existing good policies while also identifying opportunities to clarify and enhance the current ordinance. He further indicated that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be effective and proposed using development agreements as a more flexible tool to consider the unique aspects of each project.  He stated that while the county currently uses special use permits, development agreements could provide a more flexible tool for the board, noting that these agreements would enable the board to consider a wider range of variables and address the unique characteristics of each individual project.  Mr. Cooper provided an overview of the current wind energy ordinance, highlighting its comprehensive development standards. He explained that the ordinance includes numerous significant requirements for wind projects, such as proper bonding and reclamation, setbacks, joint agency approvals, and wildlife considerations. He noted that through listening sessions and public comments, they analyzed the existing ordinance to identify potential enhancements and areas for improvement.  He indicated that one focus was improving public safety involvement, and that new language is proposed to clarify their roles in inspections, post-accident reviews, decommissioning, and emergency response coordination. He indicated that it would also aim to strengthen road use agreements for construction and operational phases, formalizing the cooperation already seen between developers and the county to maintain road quality.  He noted that setbacks were a recurring concern from both the public and developers and were re-evaluated. He indicated that the current wind energy ordinance relies on peer-reviewed studies related to noise, flicker, and other factors to ensure minimum safety standards, and that while these minimum setbacks are backed by science, the ordinance allows for greater distances when needed to meet those standards. He further indicated that the proposal includes a waiver option for neighboring landowners to voluntarily allow encroachment into setback zones.  He stated that concerns about setbacks are particularly focused around communities like Heber-Overgaard, which is surrounded by U.S. Forest Service land and therefore has no room to expand outward. He indicated that maps show that even the nearest potential wind project would still be about eight to nine miles away, with no current applications pending for forest land use. He stated that other communities like Show Low and Pinetop-Lakeside are similarly restricted by surrounding forests and lack of infrastructure, further limiting the potential for nearby wind projects. Noting that emergency agencies in the southern parts of the county acknowledged these constraints but expressed a willingness to remain engaged partners in the process.  Chairman Whiting shared his appreciation for the map presentation, noting that it helps address community concerns about wind turbine proximity. He observed that many people who have expressed worries about projects being in their "backyard" can now see from the maps that their communities are actually protected by forest service buffers.  Mr. Cooper stated that, in response to community and municipal feedback, the recommendation was made not to impose strict mandatory setbacks between proposed energy projects and nearby towns or cities, noting that instead, developers would be required to consult with municipalities and tribal entities within five miles of a proposed project. He indicated that this would give local governments early input on project design and potential impacts.  He stated that environmental protection was another major focus, with continued coordination with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department. He indicated that the recommendation includes requiring the use of the best available, commercially proven technology to minimize impacts on wildlife, building on the existing rule that prohibits experimental or prototype equipment in the county.  He stated that another key recommendation is the use of development agreements instead of standard special use permits, noting that this approach allows for more flexible, case-by-case evaluations of proposed projects, taking into account local environmental concerns, land use, and potential community benefits. He indicated that these agreements would be formal contracts approved by the board and recorded with the county, enabling negotiation of specific outcomes and benefits tailored to each project rather than relying solely on check-list-style permit approvals.  Supervisor Seymore shared that he likes the development agreements, noting that it would add flexibility and strength.  Mr. Cooper discussed the current lack of a solar ordinance, explaining that they currently follow industry standards and mirror wind energy permit approval processes. He recommended either creating a separate solar ordinance or developing a comprehensive renewable energy ordinance with distinct sections for solar and wind. He suggested that the solar standards would largely mirror wind energy ordinance components, such as decommissioning bonds and environmental studies, while also accounting for the unique characteristics of solar projects. He highlighted the need to formalize review processes and potentially use development agreements to provide flexibility in project evaluation. Supervisor Seymore  emphasized the importance of moving forward with the recommendations. He shared his appreciation for the comprehensive study and the work done by staff and partners during the listening sessions. He stated that while they couldn't make a motion at this time, he wanted to direct staff to proceed with implementing the ordinance changes as quickly as possible, noting that the goal was to create a framework that would enable communities to benefit from renewable energy projects while maintaining proper oversight and consideration of community concerns.  Chairman Whiting echoed Supervisor Seymour's sentiments, praising the thorough approach to developing the renewable energy ordinance. He highlighted the importance of the county's process, which involved gathering public input, consulting stakeholders, and carefully evaluating concerns.  He shared his appreciation for the team investigating which issues were within their jurisdiction, fact-checked concerns, and developed recommendations accordingly. He shared that he felt the community was well heard and was proud of the work done by the team. He indicated that he agrees about moving forward with the recommendations and creating an ordinance that serves the county's citizens effectively.  Supervisor Whitesinger shared her appreciation for the public participation in the process. She noted the significant number of comments received and emphasized the value of community members taking time to educate the board about their perspectives. She highlighted the importance of recognizing and respecting the effort citizens make to attend meetings, share concerns, and help the board understand different viewpoints. She stressed that when individuals take time away from their families and personal commitments to participate, it signals the importance of the issue to them. She noted that she valued the community's input in helping the board investigate and understand complex issues related to renewable energy development.  Mr. Layton thanked the board for the discussion and invited board members to reach out if they have any additional thoughts or comments. He explained that staff will develop specific language for the ordinance revisions based on the recommendations presented. He indicated that they would investigate the proposed changes and work to move the process forward.
Chairman Whiting inquired about the timeline for bringing the proposed ordinance revisions back to the board for consideration.
Mr. Cooper stated that due to statutory requirements for ordinance changes, the fastest timeline would be approximately two months, noting that this includes necessary steps like advertising requirements and obtaining a recommendation from the Planning and Zoning Commission. He emphasized they would move the process forward as expeditiously as possible while adhering to state law.
