PUBLIC HEARING : Consideration and discussion of the proposed Jail District, reaffirming Resolution Number 12-18 , establishing a Jail District in Navajo County for the purpose of acquiring, constructing, operating, maintaining and financing county jails and jail systems and amending Section 2 Sentence 3 of the Resolution to read "subsection A and section 42-5352" instead of "subsection A and section 42-4342":
Chairman Williams:   We'll go back to Item 3a, public hearing: Consideration and discussion of the proposed Jail District, reaffirming Resolution Number 12-18 , establishing a Jail District in Navajo County for the purpose of acquiring, constructing, operating, maintaining and financing county jails and jail systems.  Sheriff Clark and Bryan Layton.
Sheriff Clark :  Good morning, Chairman, Board.  KC Clark, Navajo County Sheriff.  I'll speak after Bryan.
Bryan Layton :  Chairman and Supervisors, I appreciate the time.  Today we have the public hearing for the proposed jail district.  As required by statute, it was advertised in the newspaper, and we also took the opportunity to post this in libraries and post offices where appropriate to try to get the word out.  And as we've been giving presentations and sharing with the public, we've also taken the opportunity to try to share this date, and we encourage those with their feelings and opinions to come and share those today.  What I'd like to do real quick is just share again some of the details and the context to the jail district and why we're at the place we're at right now.  And, primarily, it starts with the recession.  The County's response during the recession I think is something that we can be very proud of.  During that time, revenues dropped, costs went up, the State shared costs with us.  And, for the most part, the County adjusted internally for that.  We reduced staffing by sixteen percent (16%), we changed how we conducted business.  And as I shared before, having the opportunity to tighten your belt and reexamine your expenditures is not a bad thing.  On the other hand, we do need to remember the impact on staff.  So, during this time, staff wages were very stagnant, and we also asked staff to contribute more to healthcare.  And so while the public didn't feel this a lot, it was the staff that really took this challenge on, and I think it's something that we can be very proud of.  This last last year, though, was different for us.  You'll recall that we needed to cut expenditures again.  We cut and trimmed as much as we could, but we got to a point where we just did not see any more opportunities to cut positions and individual little things, and we were forced to consider cutting entire programs.  This is when we closed the juvenile detention facility and the County Attorney's Child Support Program.  And these were changes that the public started to really feel.  And so we're entering a new phase where the County has cut and trimmed about as much as it can maintaining services.  And now we're into having to consider cutting programs.  And during this time, we were able to avoid job loss in public safety with deputies and detention officers.  And the Sheriff has been a fantastic partner during this time period.  He's pursued federal inmate revenues which has been critical to the County.  His office and his staff have done without and changed, so his office has been a fantastic partner.  But all of the job loss that we've had have been outside of public safety, which has been very deliberate and I think something we can be proud of.  And this year, as you recall, as we started this year we had a good year.  State revenues started to come back.  We were able to keep a little bit more cash on hand in our reserves than we thought possible.  But as a reminder, you'll see that law enforcement and criminal justice, so that's prosecution, defense, probation, the jail, that's over half of our budget.  And then you'll see that we were able to maintain our standard for our financial reserves at ten percent (10%).

So, as we've talked about the closure of NGS and the Cholla Power Plant, it's a whole new time for us.  After a long time where we've cut and cut, we're now entering a time when we have to cut an additional two-and-a-half million on top of what we've already cut for the last decade.  Kind of geographically, I've changed this up based on some feedback that we got, so if you put that graphically and you add up, you'd have to put in the Recorder, the Superintendent of Schools, the Treasurer, the Assessor, put all of those departments together to equal two-and-a-half million.  And at that point, you're talking about all of the land valuation, everything that has to do with real estate transactions, all of the support that we provide the schools, a lot of these big services that our residents depend on every day.  Sometimes they don't even realize it, but they do depend on these services to conduct business and in our schools.  And we've shared this graphic with our residents.  During the recession, we've trimmed, we've cut.  We now have a very lean, small workforce.  But with the loss of coal revenues, we would be looking at losing an additional 20 to 25 percent of our workforce.  So, how do you do the same amount of work with 60 people that was done prior with 100 people?  That's helped putting that in perspective.  And so we've asked the question, you know, how does Navajo County change.  And as we've discussed, additional job loss would have to include public safety at this point.  And we've talked to the Sheriff, we'd be looking in terms of eight patrol deputies, additionally 13 detention officers, four to five support staff.  So it really adds up.  That impacts the Sheriff's ability to collect federal inmate revenues and be that resource for our federal partners.  It will greatly impact our response times, our capital needs in public safety and the Sheriff's ability to get out there, be there and respond.  And we're also concerned about case loads of our investigators and our prosecutors.  And so can we properly pursue justice for our residents?  And, also, our community becomes less business and developer friendly.  We talked about the impacts to the Assessor, to the Recorder.  When folks conduct  a real estate transaction, a lot of times they're interfacing with the title company,  That title company, in reality, is interfacing with all of the services that are right down this hall and the staff that are down this hall, so we become much less responsive.  Items that usually take same day service or next day could be weeks or a month.  And, also, all services would slow down as all departments reduce more and more staff.

So as we discussed, there are some options moving forward.  Right now, the budget is balanced.  We have revenues that meet our expenditure needs, and we've been able to put some money aside, carry it forward, which is proper and appropriate for us.  With the loss of that two-and-a-half million, it puts us out of balance.  You know, the option there is to just cut expenditures.  That's a very tough ask.  That would be a lot of change for our residents and for our staff.  And that's what we've been calling a forced reduction of services.  That would put us into balance.  We can replace those lost revenues.  The one opportunity that we do have that we've identified is a jail district.  So a jail district is not intended for new jail construction.  It's intended for the daily operation of the jail.  It's a one-third cent sales tax.  It allows our visitors, everyone who passes through on I-40 and up to participate in that.  Some folks have asked, you know, why a jail tax.  It's one of our largest expenses, and it's the largest expense within public safety.   That would balance us.  It's not a financial windfall for the County, but it helps fill the gap for what we're losing in coal and puts us on a right track.  It's used by eight other counties, and it also eliminates jail fees.  And we've been asked about this a lot.  So cities and towns currently pay a jail fee for those who they wish to incarcerate, charge for misdemeanors.  The Sheriff has to take everyone with a felony, but misdemeanors is up to the cities and towns.  You'll see at the bottom of the chart there, each amount in 2017 that the cities and towns paid in jail fees.  Those would go away.  The Sheriff would not charge those at that point, and it would be written into the language of the jail district.  So those would go away.  That would mean money going directly back into cities and towns.  If you look at over the 20-year term of the jail district, that's eight-and-a-half million dollars based on recent spending.  So there would be no more jail fees for cities and towns.  And as far as impact to individuals, we've provided some examples.  We've shared these with the public.  For a lot of your daily purchases around town, it's a couple of cents.  And as you get into our visitors coming in, we would charge a little bit more on hotels and your daily goods.  So that's a brief recap of the context of the jail district, and I'll turn the time over to the Sheriff now to share his thoughts.
Chairman Williams:  Thank you, Bryan.
Sheriff Clark:   Again, thank you for the time today, Chairman, Board members.  You know, as I came a few weeks ago and we talked about 31 years I've been doing this, made life and death decisions and on and on.  It's very expensive.  But we have a safe community.  If you watch the news, we don't have the problems in Navajo County or, pretty much, in Arizona that you see across the nation where you see these horrific videos and so forth.  We've done well here, and that's due to all of us working together to keep us safe, keep costs down.  In the ten years since I've been Sheriff, I have never gone over budget  This is not a windfall to me in any way.  I have never gone over budget.  I've never come in ten years and asked the Board for additional people.  What I've done is taken some of the command staff and got rid of those positions and made it into deputy positions and so forth.  There's been times within the last ten years where I'm absolutely embarrassed to walk into the jail have you taken into consideration that when the power plant closes, the additional businesses that are going to go out of business here in the Holbrook, Winslow, Joe City area, Mr. G's or so forth?  I work with, and I'm happy they did.  It's one of the few things I think they've done well for Navajo County.  I'm taking a pot shot real quick at the State.  But they've done well, they've helped us, they've given us that opportunity.

You know, one of the other things I've heard is, well, what about if you get this through, then you're going to come back and charge us more?  You know, 31 years, ten years as Sheriff, I've never seen the County be not a good partner to our cities and towns.  As a matter of fact, every time they've asked for something, we go back and help.  We're good partners.  We don't go back on our word.  It's my understanding that there's a law that says we can't.  But I don't think we need  a law.  We're not going to go back and charge the cities.  That's just not what Navajo County does. You know, a couple of things I've heard, well, charge the inmates more.  I do.  I mean, Sheriff Arpaio gets all the credit, but we do quite a bit here, too.  One of the things that we started doing as I've become Sheriff, each time an inmate goes to a medical unit, we charge him $10.00; for pharmacy service, another $10.00; $2.50 for inmate property locks; replacement fee of $2.50; $5.00 for their bracelets; $1.50 a day for their meals; $25.00 hospital transport fee; 50 cents for copy service per document; $5.00 notary service.  You can say, well, how do you collect that?  Well, a lot of these people come in, and they have money in their pocket, and they have a ton of money to buy honey buns, skittles, so forth.  I charge that fee first.  If they run out of money, then by statute, I have to give them a lot of that stuff.  But most of the people that come to our jail come back repeatedly, so they come back a year later with a pocket full of money, we track that, and the first thing I do is take out what they owe the County.

You know, one of the cheapest ways to reduce the service of incarceration, public safety, is to make sure that we try to work with these folks and get rid of recidivism or lower it.  Ten years ago, when I ran for Sheriff, one of the people that I ran against, we were talking, there were problems with the budget back then, and this person said, oh, let's do a bake sale.  And I thought, well, for goodness sake, how immature is that?  Really?  You want to run for Sheriff, and you want to do bake sales.  Folks, I'm selling Dilly Bars now.  I'm selling Dilly Bars.  It's incredible.  Dilly Bars and ice cream sandwiches to put in our inmate commissary fund so I can have our programs director continue teaching GED and so forth.  Because a GED is just a GED, but I'll tell you what, that's a start.  It gets people a job somewhere.  You have to have a GED to work anywhere in this county, Navajo County.  So we're working on recidivism.  So, again, a third of a cent, I get it.  I think there's somewhere, we don't have the exact numbers, but there's 112,000 people that live in this county, total.  And I believe I've been told that there's 50- to 60,000 property owners, 50- to 60,000.  Those are the people that are paying right now for that jail.  If we do the sales tax, again, my numbers could be off, but I was told it's going to cost approximately the average family in Navajo County $20.00 or $30.00 a year.  $20.00 or $30.00 is a lot of money if you don't have it, but to guard your family, to make sure they're safe, what's the cost?  So I would ask that people vote their conscience.

You know, one of the things on the ballot would be Proposition 419.  Typically, the Governor will be the top spot on there and probably Proposition 419 will be on the back of the ballot.  You  might miss it.  I would ask people whatever you do, for or against, go all of the way down your ballot, look for it.  You know, I can tell you the different things we've done across the county.  I mean, Holbrook needed assistance, we gave them three deputies; Winslow had a shooting over there, I think their second shooting, there's been three now, we did the investigation for them.  Mr. Holmes was talking about the Heber-Overgaard parade and so forth, that takes a ton of manpower from the Sheriff's Office.  We have almost 24-hour coverage.  Burglaries, just in the Heber-Overgaard area, are seventy-six percent (76%) down since 2012.  I have some other numbers there.  It's great stuff.  We've done more proactive stuff than what we did in the past.  I would hate to go back on that.  So, thank you for your time, and I'll answer questions.
