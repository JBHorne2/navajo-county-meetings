Chairman Seymore greeted the Board and explained that the Board received a letter

Celia Laughlin stated that Secretary Fontes’ letter on May 23, 2024, states that ARS
16-602 requires only a limited post-election hand count audit, not full manual tabulation
and that ARS 16-621C provides a clear exception that hand tabulation is permitted
when electronic tabulation becomes impractical. Ms. Laughlin claimed that that
threshold had been reached in Navajo County.  She claimed that 117.4% of voter
registration rates in Excel logs have been hidden from public view, refusal of the citizen
funded forensic audit offered in spring of 2024, unverifiable logic and accuracy tests,
observers witnessing printers operating feet from central computers with no modem
checks, denied machine inspections, broken seals, unlocked ballot handoffs, observer
exclusions, and unfulfilled public records requests are systemic failures that render the
ESNS machines impractical for secure tabulation.  Ms. Laughlin requested that the
Board declare electronic tabulation impractical for 2026 and to direct the immediate
planning of precinct level hand counts or hand counts at every voting center. Ms.
Laughlin informed the Board that her group is collecting signatures for a 2026 citizen
initiative to make precinct hand counts the law of Arizona. 
Donna Wastchak-Shurwin greeted the Board and stated that she has lived in Navajo
County for 31 years and has been involved in the Election Integrity Network Group for 2
years.  Ms. Shurwin asked the Board why Navajo County does not publicly advertise
the need for poll workers, stating that she feels that it’s an important opportunity that
every Navajo County citizen should have made available to them.  Ms. Shurwin
recognized that there were a lot of young people in the audience and stressed the
importance of them being able to participate in elections. Ms. Shurwin stated that in
2024 she offered to help the County find poll workers when Christen Haddox told her
that she is challenged at each election to find poll workers.  She explained that her
efforts have been shunned and undermined because most people she had referred,
were ignored.  Ms. Shurwin expressed disappointment in Navajo County at how they
spent grant money from the Center for Tech and Civic Life to build a new Elections
Warehouse without installing quality video cameras for monitoring elections, and
instead, installed low-resolution fisheye cameras without audio recording equipment.

Steve Carvajal greeted the Board stating that he is from Show Low. He said he wanted
to talk to the Board about wind farms and special use permits.  He stated his opinion
that the County does not have sufficient paperwork requirements in the permitting
process.  Mr. Carvajal said that 3 entities work together, the developer, the constructor,
and the operating company.  Mr. Carvajal claimed that the operating company, the one
who will be paying the taxes, has not been brought to the table yet.  Mr. Carvajal shared
his belief that there are 15-17 of these operations scheduled and that he does not
believe that 17 multi-billion-dollar companies will come in to buy the operations.  He
shared his opinion that the permitting process with planning and development is
inadequate because it does not require the operating company to be part of the
process.  He claimed that if an operator does not purchase the development projects,
then there will be unused monstrosities ruining the landscape.  Mr. Carvajal implored
the Board to change the requirements for permits to include an operating company and
a 10-year minimum bond.
Belinda Quog greeted the Board and stated that as the 2026 election cycle approaches,
she would like to remind the Board of the County’s perceived negligence in the 2024
election cycle.  Ms. Quog expressed her belief that the 2024 primary should not have
been legally certified as the margins of error and lack of due diligence were shameful.
She urged the Board to put forth stronger efforts to successfully perform a lawfully
processed election for 2026.  Ms. Quog shared what she perceived as errors with the
previous elections, such as; recorded broken chain of custody events, ballot box
camera malfunctions, zip drives and vote information left in kiosk machines delivered to
precincts, antiquated logic and accuracy tests, ballot and voting observers made to sit
on opposite side of the rooms and unable to observe, incorrect number of party
representatives at precincts, precincts with zero voters, hundreds of voters turned away,
election workers confirming that provisional votes aren’t counted, boxes of open ballots,
deputies picking up precinct reports that were insufficiently filled or not filled out, using
uncertified machines, refusal of a highly qualified professional to audit the machines,
Rayleen Richards, Christen Haddox, and a minor adjudicating ballots on a Sunday with
no witnesses, unethically asking poll workers for their affiliation and selectively ousting
mostly republican people, and refusal of qualified individuals to work on the
reservation.  Ms. Quog asked the Board to do their due diligence for 2026 and make
sure that none of the issues she brought up occur.
Chairman Seymore asked staff to look into the items that had been brought up in the
call to the public, specifically the items on development agreements, elections, and poll
workers.

SUPERINTENDENT OF SCHOOLS: 
a. Consideration and approval of the recommended distribution of Secure Rural
Schools (SRS) Title I funds to school districts.
A presentation was shown.
Rachael Lewis, Superintendent of Schools, presented information regarding the
Secure Rural Schools (SRS) Title I, II, and III funds. Ms. Lewis stated that she
wanted to provide background and education on the purpose and history of the
Secure Rural Schools program. Ms. Lewis explained that in 1908, Congress
committed to sharing 25% of federal timber harvest revenues with local schools
and rural governments. She stated that this system functioned until the
mid-1990s, when endangered species protections significantly reduced timber
harvests, leaving schools without sufficient revenue. Congress then enacted the
Secure Rural Schools and County Self-Determination Act to compensate for
declining timber receipts. She noted that timber harvests have not recovered and
that in years when SRS was not reauthorized, Navajo County received
only approximately $55,000 to distribute among roads, Firewise communities,
and schools. She described the challenges caused by inconsistent federal
reauthorization, explaining that schools struggle to plan and hire long-term staff
when funding is uncertain. She shared that superintendents use SRS funds for
transportation needs, including bus purchases and repairs, as well as
after-school tutoring programs, teacher stipends, and advanced coursework
opportunities. She emphasized the importance of classroom aides, particularly in
early grades, and noted that these funds help districts maintain essential support
positions. Ms. Lewis reported that through lobbying efforts by elected officials,
including members of the Board, Secure Rural Schools was reauthorized and
backdated for fiscal years 2024, 2025, and 2026, though the authorization
expires again in September. She reviewed the current allocation, noting that the
County received $754,432, with $25,000 designated for roads, a portion for
Firewise communities, and the majority for schools. She explained that $50,000
is distributed to the Navajo County Accommodation District, and the remaining
funds are allocated to districts based on average daily membership and the
number of eligible forest acres. She emphasized that districts use these funds
responsibly and carefully, especially given ongoing uncertainty. She stated that
FY25 funds are expected in mid-April with a similar allocation and will be
submitted through the Consent Agenda, and FY26 funds are anticipated in
mid-April 2027. Ms. Lewis concluded by thanking the Board for the opportunity to
present and for their support, stating that she appreciates the chance to educate
the public and has gained a deeper understanding of school needs through this
process.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger made a motion to approve the recommended
distribution of Secure Rural Schools (SRS) Title I funds to school districts, motion
seconded by Supervisor Whiting.  Vote unanimous approving the motion.

completion of the Obstruction Evaluation Airport Airspace Analysis. He noted
that these steps can only occur once the wind farm is fully constructed and
commercially operational. Mr. Cooper reported that although construction is
complete, the West Camp Wind Farm is not yet in commercial operation. The
company is currently anticipating to be operational no later than June 2026 and
is attempting to expedite the timeline. Mr. Cooper emphasized that FAA
requirements cannot be overridden by the County and that activation of the
ADLS is dependent on federal processes and grid-connection timelines. Once
FAA testing and approvals are complete, the ADLS will be activated, and the
obstruction lights will illuminate only when aircraft are detected within the
system’s parameters.
Chairman Seymore thanked staff for looking into the matter.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger asked for clarification, confirming that the
obstruction lighting is currently operating continuously. She further inquired that,
upon the anticipated June activation of the Aircraft Detection Lighting System,
the lights would only illuminate when an aircraft is within the required detection
radius and asked how many miles that coverage area includes.
Mr. Cooper responded that the obstruction lighting is currently operating
continuously because the Aircraft Detection Lighting System has not yet been
authorized for full commissioning. Staff explained that the FAA still requires the
lights to remain active for pilot safety while the towers are within navigable
airspace and until all FAA testing and approvals are complete. He further
explained that once the new system is activated, it will include a horizontal
detection buffer of three nautical miles in all directions around the wind farm. If
an aircraft is detected within that three-mile radius and at an altitude of 200 feet

Approval of the Re-Entry Program Modernization Project for Navajo County Jail
Background:
Navajo County initiated a project to modernize their Re-Entry Program with the aim of addressing the
current and future needs of the program initiatives. Navajo County has engaged Kyndryl to build onto the
enterprise framework with Yavapai County. This implementation will configure the application for
Navajo County's specific workflows, data requirements, and system integrations. The purpose of this
project is to develop a statewide cross-system, recidivism tracking database that will support all counties
to identify people with social determinants of health, mental health, and substance use disorders at the
earliest possible criminal justice intercept and link them to care to prevent them from re-offending.
Navajo County will incur a monthly recurring charge not to exceed $3,293 which will be paid for out of
funds provided by Yavapai County. There will be no fiscal impact on Navajo County during the initial
start-up. Any subsequent maintenance fees associated with the database will be paid for by grant and/or
appropriation monies.

Kyndryl supports two modes of execution for security patches 
implementation:  
• Ad hoc or “Patch Now” patching. In this mode, updates are made 
directly to the EC2 instances. This mode works best for mutable 
infrastructures, where instances cannot be destroyed without data loss or 
are not deployed using auto-scaling availability groups. Kyndryl uses the 
native AWS Systems Manager Patch Manager to implement critical and 
security patches on the supported OS versions / distributions.  
• EC2 image update patching. In this mode, EC2 instances are updated 
by replacing them with an updated Amazon image that is patched with 
the latest security updates. This mode works better for immutable 
infrastructures, where instances can be destroyed without data loss and 
are deployed using auto-scaling groups. Updated Amazon images are 
released regularly by AWS or can be provided by Navajo County or by 
Kyndryl (when Amazon image lifecycle add-on service is contracted).  
 
When patches fail to install, Kyndryl will work on remediating the issue during the 
maintenance window and will contact Navajo County if their intervention is required. If not 
solved during this timeframe, the issue is documented, and an attempt to patch the instance 
will be made again during the next cycle. Execution results for both successful and failed 
patches are reported after each cycle. Kyndryl also provides a monthly report that includes:  
• Summary of the security patching profile agreed to with Navajo County, which includes 
instances in scope, exemptions to the default patch baseline, and the agreed -upon 
maintenance of windows and cycles. 
• Major vulnerabilities and events that occurred during the reporting period, and the status / 
result of the corresponding ad hoc maintenance plan, if applicable. 
• List of instances that missed patches during the last maintenance window execution .

• Agree on ad hoc maintenance processes for high priority and emergency security patches.

Security Compliance Management 
Kyndryl will use AWS Security Hub and AWS Inspector to provide Navajo County with a 
comprehensive view of the security state in AWS and will help check the environment against 
security industry standards and best practices. Security Hub: integrates with other native 
services such as AWS Config, AWS GuardDuty, Amazon Inspector, and AWS Health; collects 
security data from Navajo County AWS accounts; and helps Navajo County analyze security 
trends and identify the highest priority security issues. The defined rules will be applied during 
the implementation phase or during the onboarding phase as per the contract.  
Kyndryl Responsibilities  
Kyndryl will verify that native AWS Security Hub consumes, aggregates, and analyzes 
security findings from various supported AWS and third-party products. Security Hub will also 
generate its own findings by running automated and continuous checks against the rules in a 
set of supported security standards. These rules will determine whether controls within a 
standard are being adhered to. Security Hub provides controls for the following standards:  
• CIS AWS Foundations Benchmark v1.4.0. 
• AWS Foundational Security Best Practices v1.0.0. 
Kyndryl will use AWS Security Hub to define, audit, and enforce secure configurations of 
AWS resources. AWS Inspector will be used to configure compliance controls for guest 
operating systems. AWS GuardDuty will be used to define the compliance configuration for 
the cloud platform. 
Kyndryl will implement the Regulatory Compliance built-in initiative, which continuously 
assesses and analyzes the controls for cloud security. This implementation covers security 
compliance controls across the following resource types:  
• Identity and Access Management. 
• Storage. 
• Database services. 
• Logging and monitoring. 
• Networking. 
• EC2 instances. 
• Other services (CloudTrail, KMS).

1. Project Description 
Navajo County initiated a project to modernize their Re-Entry Program (“Project”), with the 
aim of addressing the current and future needs of the Navajo County and partner county 
program initiatives. Navajo County has engaged Kyndryl to build onto the enterprise 
framework developed with Yavapai County. This implementation will configure the application 
for Navajo County’s specific workflows, data requirements, and system integrations 
(“Services”).  
1.1 Purpose 
The purpose of the Project is to develop a statewide cross-system, recidivism tracking 
database that will support all counties to identify people with social determinants of health, 
mental health, and substance use disorders at the earliest possible criminal justice intercept 
and link them to care to prevent them from re-offending. Focus areas: 
• Connecting people with appropriate services. 
• Keeping people out of the system (recidivate/re-treads). 
• Minimizing the number of people who get lost in the system. 
• Results connected to services (referral) and diverted to services, into actual treatment. 
 
1.2 Project Goals 
Navajo County’s objective is to deploy the Re-Entry Program by implementing a scalable, 
configurable, and user-friendly solution. Key goals: 
• Leveraging modern, cloud-based technologies for scalability and flexibility. 
• Enhancing the user experience for program staff and partners. 
• Enabling non-programmatic configuration to reduce reliance on custom development. 
• Improving access to actionable reporting and performance metrics. 
• Supporting secure, permission-based data sharing across agencies and jurisdictions. 
2. Project Scope

the enterprise framework previously implemented in Yavapai County. Each deployment will 
support the core modules of the application (screening, referral tracking, case management,

Resolution 12-2026, supporting the Sheriff's Office to request $150,000 from Gila River Indian
Community grant funds for its accreditation management project
Background:
The passage of Proposition 202 by the voters of Arizona in November 2002 set the stage for new gaming
compacts between the State and the respective tribes. An important provision of Proposition 202 is the
sharing of gaming revenues with the State, allowing a portion of the shared revenue to be retained and
distributed by a tribe itself. The Gila River Indian Community (GRIC) funds Arizona municipalities in
providing government services in the areas of economic development, education, healthcare, public safety
and transportation.
The Arizona Law Enforcement Accreditation Program (ALEAP) strives to enhance public trust, ensure
operational excellence, and advance the quality of law enforcement services through its rigorous
accreditation process. Navajo County Sheriff’s Office (NCSO), accredited by the ALEAP, is committed
to maintaining its highest standards of professionalism, integrity, and effectiveness. Funding from GRIC
helps NCSO’s continued effort in managing the required tasks in a timely manner and maintaining the
accreditation for two years.

4. Describe how the proposed project satisfies one or more of the priority funding areas identified by the Gila River

This proposed project satisfies the priority funding area associated with Public Safety. As indicated above,

started in the District. A Type 6 wildland fire engine is a versatile and highly maneuverable firefighting vehicle 
that is designed to operate in rugged terrain and narrow roads. It is smaller than larger fire engines and can 
access areas that are inaccessible to larger engines. The Show Low area is characterized by narrow roads, 
steep slopes, and dense vegetation, which makes it challenging for larger firefighting vehicles to maneuver. 
A Type 6 engine is an essential piece of equipment that can provide fast and effective response to wildfires 
in the area and allows fire personnel to be able to catch fires while they are smaller before they become 
established and to large to stop. In summary, without this equipment, and the ability it affords to 
aggressively and quickly attack fires, the public and the community face a significant risk of a wildland fire 
becoming established and unstoppable and running into the communities of the White Mountains. TMFMD 
has been severely limited in it's ability to make capital purchases and currently, most of the type 6's in service 
in the District are aged and past the NFPA recommended useful life of 20 years.

Submitted For: Grant Evans, Director of Facilities and Capital Projects

Motion before the Board:
Consideration of accepting a State Grants-In-Aid (SGIA) Construction funding in the amount of
$50,000.00 for Pinedale Public Library Roof & HVAC Replacement Project and authorizing staff to sign
grant agreement documents. 
Background:
Pinedale Public Library was established in 2011 when the community leased the building from Navajo
County. In 2014, the Library became part of the County Library District. The collection of the Library
contains 10,282 volumes. The Library circulates 1,010 items per year. It is a volunteer-run library that
provides equitable access to books, digital resources, and curated information to all community members.
The Library serves a cultural hub hosting events, workshops, and author events. In 2023, the Pinedale
Library was recognized by Arizona Library Association for the Volunteer Library of the Year. 
The county-owned building needs long-overdue facility improvements. The Navajo County Library
District and Facility Management Departments collaborated to apply for FY2026 State Grants-in-Aid
(SGIA) Construction grant funds administered by the Arizona State Library, Archives and Public Records
Office. In 1981, the Arizona legislature developed SGIA to help Arizona libraries meet the information
needs of Arizona residents. SGIA provides construction funding to public libraries, and annual awards to
county libraries and to municipal libraries in cities with populations of 100,000 or more. The County's
application has been approved with funding in the amount of $50,000. 
The project addresses the library’s roof issues, including potential water damage, structural weakening,
and mold growth by removing overdue roof and replacing with new energy efficient roofing materials.
The existing roof is over 25 years old. 
The project also removes the two existing gas pack, packaged rooftop HVAC units. They are over 25
years old and use R-22 refrigerant. This refrigerant is phased out. It is cost prohibitive to obtain when we
need to recharge or repair the units. The project will install two ducted mini-split HVAC systems. The
equipment will help us achieve a cost effective and energy efficient goal, saving the County meaningful
money on our monthly energy bill.
It is a dollar-to-dollar matching grant, and the local match comes from the County's Building funds. The
grant performance period ends on June 30, 2027. The County's Administration Office ensures compliance
with the grant's terms and conditions.
