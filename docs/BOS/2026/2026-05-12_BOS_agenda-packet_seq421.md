CALL TO THE PUBLIC:
Celia Laughlin addressed the Board and shared that for two years the citizens of Navajo
County have shared their concerns regarding election administration and economic
development. She stated that citizens have submitted affidavits and public records over
the past two years outlining what they believe are significant issues, including chain of
custody problems, unauthorized ballot processing, observer access concerns, and the
denial of a citizen funded audit. She also cited a recent voter registration confirmation
notice as evidence of inaccurate voter rolls in violation of ARS 16-1605 and 16-1606.
She asked that the 7-step Corrective Action Plan provided to the Board be addressed on
a future agenda. She additionally raised concerns about Real AZ, which she stated
continued collecting funds after dissolution. She requested that the County support
training of local students to serve as clerks who can help count votes and that County
Attorney Brad Carlyon be placed on a future agenda to address questions regarding
prosecution practices and election related legal matters. Ms. Laughlin concluded by
stating that citizens are prepared to assist with lawful corrective actions.

Susan Clark thanked the Board for the opportunity to speak and stated that she and her
family are opposed to Item 10a, the West Camp II development agreement. She
explained that when they purchased their home on Hodges Lane in 2016, the nearest
wind turbine was approximately five miles from their property. She stated that turbines
are now located about two miles away, and if the proposed agreement is approved, the
nearest turbine would be approximately one mile from their home. Ms. Clark stated that
her family moved to the area for its desert views and pristine surroundings and that they
accepted the presence of turbines at a five-mile distance. She expressed concern that
the number and proximity of turbines have increased significantly and that they are now
clearly visible both during the day and at night due to the red blinking lights. She also
referenced concerns about vibrations and electromagnetic fields emitted from the
turbines. She stated that at a meeting she attended, she was told these were not issues.
However, she later received an email inviting her to become a partner in the project,
offering monetary compensation in exchange for easements and a release of liability
related to vibrations, electromagnetic fields, and land movement. Ms. Clark noted that
these easements would remain in effect for the life of the project, up to 25 years, even if
the property were sold.

ADMINISTRATIVE SERVICES: 
a. Presentation/Summary of Navajo County’s Fiscal Year 2025 Annual Financial
Report, Report on Internal Control and on Compliance, and Federal Single
Audit Report
Jayson Vowell introduced Ben Hur from Fester and Chapman PLLC. and
representatives from the Office of the Auditor General appearing online, Nicole
Bartlett, Stephanie Gerber, and Carla Wall.
Carla Wall introduced herself as a technical manager with the Arizona Auditor
General and explained that the office is responsible for conducting annual
audits of all Arizona counties and that sometimes they contract out the audits,
that when they do, they select qualified CPA firms with experience in auditing
counties. She explained that for Navajo County’s fiscal year 2025 audit, Fester
and Chapman were contracted and that the office of the Attorney General
worked closely with the Fester and Chapman team throughout the audit.
A presentation was shown.
Ben Hur expressed his appreciation to the County Manager Bryan Layton and
Jayson Vowell and their teams for their help and support during the audit. Mr.
Hur then presented the audit results, stating that they issued a modified
opinion, meaning that financial statements are presented fairly in all material
aspects and in accordance with GAAP. He reported that there were 3 findings
considered significant deficiencies in internal control over financial reporting,
and that 2 of the findings related to IT controls and one for the Treasurer’s bank
reconciliation. Mr. Hur then went over the Single Audit report which is triggered
when non-federal agencies receive more than $750,000 in federal grants. They
performed an audit on 4 programs in accordance with the laws and regulation
guidelines from the federal government and issued a modified opinion on the 4
programs. They also communicated that there were two new standards
successfully implemented in FY25. He reported that there were no difficulties
encountered during the audit, and no disagreements with management and
stated that there were 5 findings included in the report, 3 related to financial

MEMORANDUM OF 
UNDERSTANDING
Prison Rape Elimination Act (PREA) Victim 
Advocacy Services
This Memorandum of Understanding (“MOU”) is entered into by and between Navajo County, a 
political subdivision of the State of Arizona (“County”), acting through its Juvenile Detention 
Center (“Detention Center”), and Northeast Arizona Family Resource Center, d/b/a Navajo 
County Family Advocacy Center, an Arizona nonprofit corporation (“FAC” or “Provider”).
This MOU is entered into to satisfy applicable requirements of the Prison Rape Elimination Act 
(“PREA”), including 28 C.F.R. § 115.353, which requires access to victim advocacy services for 
juveniles confined in juvenile detention facilities.
This MOU is effective January 1, 2026, through December 31, 2026, unless earlier terminated as 
provided herein.
1. PURPOSE
The purpose of this MOU is to establish a framework for cooperation between the County and 
FAC to ensure that detained juveniles who are victims of sexual abuse have timely access to 
victim advocacy services, consistent with PREA, the Violence Against Women Act (“VAWA”), 
and all applicable federal and state confidentiality and reporting requirements.
Nothing in this MOU is intended to create a contractual obligation for the expenditure of funds 
by either party.
2. AGENCY (COUNTY) RESPONSIBILITIES
The County, through the Navajo County Juvenile Detention Center, agrees to:
1. Make known the availability and contact information for FAC during juvenile orientation 
and through written materials provided to juveniles, guardians, staff, volunteers, and 
contractors.
2. Designate PREA-related contacts to coordinate access to advocacy services and 
transportation logistics when required:
Kirsti Hancock, PREA Coordinator
Office: 928-524-4144 | Cell: 928-241-0368
Jason Cash, Chief Probation Officer
Office: 928-524-4259
3. Maintain custody, supervision, and security of juveniles at all times. FAC shall never 
assume custody or supervisory responsibility for any juvenile.
4. Retain all responsibility and liability related to juvenile security, transportation, and 
supervision.

<!-- page 43 -->
3. PROVIDER (FAC) RESPONSIBILITIES
FAC agrees to provide victim advocacy services that it normally offers to the community, 
subject to the following limitations and conditions:
1. Scope of Services
FAC services may include confidential emotional support, crisis intervention, and 
coordination of forensic examinations performed by Sexual Assault Forensic Examiners 
(SAFEs) or Sexual Assault Nurse Examiners (SANEs), if available.
2. Confidentiality and Reporting
All services shall be provided consistent with PREA, VAWA, and applicable federal and 
state confidentiality laws and regulations. Nothing in this MOU shall be construed to 
require FAC to disclose confidential information except as required by law.
Where reporting is required, FAC shall comply with applicable reporting requirements consistent 
with law, including PREA reporting pathways. FAC may delay notification to law enforcement 
when permitted by law and necessary to protect safety, confidentiality, or the integrity of 
services.
3. Transportation Coordination
When transportation is required, FAC shall coordinate with the County’s designated 
PREA contacts. FAC shall not transport juveniles unless otherwise agreed in writing and 
consistent with law.
4. Training
FAC may, upon mutual agreement, provide training related to sexual abuse prevention 
and response. Any training shall be voluntary, subject to prior approval of content, scope, 
frequency, and cost by the County, and shall not create a funding obligation.
4. NO CUSTODY OR AGENCY RELATIONSHIP
FAC is an independent contractor. Nothing in this MOU creates an agency, partnership, joint 
venture, or employment relationship. FAC does not assume custody, control, or supervision of 
juveniles at any time.
5. INDEMNIFICATION
FAC agrees to indemnify, defend, and hold harmless Navajo County, its officers, employees, and 
agents from and against any claims, damages, liabilities, or expenses arising out of FAC’s 
negligent acts or omissions in the performance of this MOU, to the extent permitted by law.
6. INSURANCE
FAC shall maintain insurance coverage customary for organizations providing victim advocacy 
services and shall provide proof of coverage upon request.
7. NON-DISCRIMINATION
FAC shall comply with all applicable federal and state non-discrimination laws, including those 
relating to race, color, national origin, sex, disability, age, and religion.

<!-- page 44 -->
8. RECORDS RETENTION
FAC shall maintain records related to services provided under this MOU in accordance with 
applicable law and grant requirements. Confidential records shall be protected consistent with 
VAWA and PREA.
9. TERM AND TERMINATION
This MOU may be terminated by either party for convenience upon thirty (30) days’ written 
notice. Termination shall not affect obligations related to confidentiality or indemnification.
10. AMENDMENTS
This MOU may be amended only by a written instrument signed by authorized representatives of 
both parties and approved by the Navajo County Board of Supervisors.
11. NOTICES
All notices shall be in writing and delivered to the parties at their respective business addresses.
12. GOVERNING LAW
This MOU shall be governed by the laws of the State of Arizona.
SIGNATURES
This MOU is subject to approval by the Navajo County Board of Supervisors.

MEMORANDUM OF UNDERSTANDING BETWEEN YA V APAI COUNTY AND 
N AVA J O COUNTY 
 
This Memorandum of Understanding (“MOU”) is hereby made and entered into by and between 
Yavapai County through the Yavapai County Sheriff’s Office (“Yavapai”), and N AVA J O County 
through the N AVA J O County Sheriff’s Office ( “ N AVA J O ” ) 
RECITALS 
A. Yavapai and Navajo intend to work together toward the mutual goal a reduction in 
recidivism which improves public safety, population health through service linkage, 
quality of life, and retention of family and support ties for people with mental illness 
and/or are substance dependent in their respective counties and in the State of Arizona. 
Both agencies believe that implementation of a Reentry Program Database (“RPD”), as 
described herein, will further this goal. 
B. Yavapai has entered into an Agreement (“Agreement”) with Kyndryl, Inc. (“Kyndryl”), 
the developer and servicer of the RPD, which provides the ability to identify substance 
abuse and mental health issues through intake processing and entry into the database by 
program professional and County employees in order to mitigate and reduce recidivism.  
C. Yavapai received a grant from the Arizona Attorney General’s Office (“AG”) to fund this 
database program (RPD). Yavapai intends, and Navajo agrees to accept, a sub-granting of 
the original grant received by Yavapai, in order to effectuate Navajo’s ability to address 
its’ needs (programming, licensing and other related services) for the use and application 
of the RPD. The AG has approved this sub-granting and methodology for this program. 
D. Navajo desires to participate in the RPD and work within the parameters of Yavapai’s 
Agreement with Kyndryl through this MOU and the incorporation of the Agreement 
herein for reference purposes of said parameters. Navajo and Yavapai further intend for 
this MOU to revoke and supersede any prior MOUs between the parties in relation to the 
RPD and Kyndryl Agreement.

America: a Land of Many Titles 
Fire crackers light up the sky as patriotism bathes the sky in glorious red, 
white, and blue, celebrating independence and bravery. Awe fills all who watch as 
Old Glory snaps crisply in the winds of freedom, dominating the skyline as a 
symbol of strength and brotherhood. America means as much to me as it does to 
you, and I truly believe it is something to celebrate, because of our loyalty, 
ingenuity, and our diversity in life. 
America is the land of the free, but what does that truly mean? Freedom, and 
thus independence, means that we the people govern ourselves, and our affairs 
unless directly harmful to themselves or others. I firmly believe that we as 
Americans will protect this philosophy until our dying breaths, as supported by 
Nathan Hale, a Revolutionary spy as he stated, "I only regret that I have but one 
life to lose for my country" before being captured and hanged. This just highlights 
that we as Americans are fiercely protective of our ideologies and will gladly lay 
down our lives for it. America is not just the land of the free, however, is it also the 
land of opportunity. For many generations our people have migrated West with the 
idea of "manifesting destiny", an idea that is still true today. One example of this is 
the moon landing, a historical moment on July 20th. This is when American hero 
Neil Armstrong stated, "That's one small step for a man, one giant leap for 
mankind" when he first stepped on the moon. This was a significant moment for 
not just Americans, but everyone. The land of opportunity does not just refer to 
expansion and science, though, it also refers to the rich diversity we have due to 
those opportunities. Mexicans, Natives, and African Americans are just some of

' the ethnicities you can find, descended from people who moved here for job 
opportunities, or a better life. Those people bring their way of life when traveling 
here, which is what causes the diversity many see in day to day life. These are just 
some of the reasons why America is such a free and diverse country, and I am glad 
to be a part of it. 
In conclusion, America is a symbol of liberty, independence, and 
imagination. Many things support this, but a fierce protection of our freedom is a 
great way to show it. Another way you can see it is through ingenuity and 
creativity. This also feeds into how diverse America is, with many different 
cultural backgrounds settling peacefully together. This is what I believe, and with 
this essay I hope you can see it too.

By: Abby Greer 
This year I am a fourth grader. I learned about our country's Declaration of 
Independence, Constitution, and the Bill of Rights. I also learned about all the Amendments in 
the Bill of Rights. The Bill of Rights was passed in 1791. It is like a list of rules and is important 
because they protect our freedom and rights as Americans. 
Our First Amendment protects our right to freedom of speech and the right to protest. 
This year, the women's Iranian soccer team protested their country by not saluting and singing 
their national anthem at the Asian Cup. Because they protested this way, they are now getting 
death threats. In 2022, Mahsa Amini competed for Iran as a rock climber in South Korea and did 
not wear her headscarf. Because of this, she was arrested and banned from competing in other 
countries. After learning about these women, it makes me realize how important the First 
Amendment is to me. I know that me and any other American can protest about what we want, 
say what we want, and still be safe. 
The First Amendment also protects our freedom of religion. It states that we can be any 
religion we want and live in peace. In Nigeria, thousands of Christians have been killed because 
of their religion. Just this year, during Holy Week and Easter, 147 Catholics were killed. This 
makes me feel sad, angry, but also grateful. I am grateful because I get to go to a Catholic school 
and go to mass on Fridays with my school peacefully. On Sundays, I can go to church with my 
family and not be persecuted. 
Being American means having freedom and choices and not being killed or hated for 
that. It means having the ability to dance, go to school, go to mass, wear the clothes I want to 
wear, and hang out with my friends safely. I love our country and being American.

Local Innovations Spotlight - Navajo County Public Works
M
ain-
taining 
road infra-
structure 
during 
the winter 
months 
presents 
numerous 
challenges, 
particularly 
for crews tasked with filling potholes and perform-
ing asphalt concrete patchwork across the state. 
For those working in Navajo County, freezing tem-
peratures and regular snowfall complicate these 
essential tasks, as asphalt mixes must remain at 
an optimal temperature to be workable. The prob-
lem was, in order to maintain the temperature of 
the mixes, Navajo County’s crews working in these 
conditions would have to constantly reheat their 
mixes using blow torches or other external heating 
devices which ultimately slowed down operations 
and required calling in additional crew members 
to operate those external heating devices. 
Seeking a cost-effective and efficient solution, 
Navajo County’s Public Works Department ex-
plored purchasing an existing piece of equipment 
designed to address this issue. However , no com-
mercially available option met the department’s 
specific needs and requirements. Rather than 
settle for an imperfect solution, the Public Works 
Fleet Maintenance Shop leveraged their existing, 
in-house resources to design and develop a cus-
tom device.
The Shop team repurposed an old asphalt 
spreader box attachment, no longer in use, and 
transformed it into a ‘cold mix hot plate box 
attachment’ . Among various modifications, they 
achieved this by equipping the old spreader box 
with propane burners, resulting in a heating 
system that could be fitted onto the department’s 
standard 1.5 ton pickup-trucks typically utilized 
by their crews performing asphalt patchwork. The 
entire process, including installation, fabrication, 
and painting, was completed by the Fleet Mainte-
nance Shop staff. 
The benefits of this new device have been imme-
diate and significant. Navajo County PW crews can 
now efficiently perform road maintenance with-
out the constant need for clunky external heating 
methods resulting 
in faster and high-
er-quality repairs. 
Additionally, their 
material waste has 
been minimized, 
and fewer person-
nel are required for 
each operation, al-
lowing for better allocation of workforce resources. 
Beyond operational efficiency, the time now saved 
on each project has meant crews spend less time 
in the freezing cold, which has contributed to im-
proved morale and overall safer work conditions. 
Financially, the custom-built approach proved 
to be a cost-effective alternative to purchasing 
a new device. The total cost of implementing 
this innovative solution amounted to just over 
$5,800, including labor , parts and materials. In 
contrast, during their search for commercially 
available solutions, their search indicated that a 
similar product - which again would likely not be 
suited to their needs without additional custom-
ization - would have cost approximately $13,000 
or more. By repurposing existing equipment and 
utilizing in-house expertise, Navajo County Public 
Works achieved significant savings while im-
proving road maintenance 
capabilities. 
This project exemplifies the 
power of innovation and 
resourcefulness within local 
transportation agencies. By 
thinking outside the box 
and maximizing available 
resources, Navajo County 
Public Works has set a precedent for efficient, 
cost-effective problem solving that benefits not 
only their agency but perhaps most importantly, 
the community they serve.

Program Background
3
The Federal Highway Administration established the 
Technical Assistance Program for Local and Tribal 
agencies (LTAP & TTAP) in 1981.
The mission was to help local municipal agencies leverage 
existing resources in order to: 
➔ Respond efficiently to and cope with transportation 
problems through technical assistance; and
➔ Increase workforce expertise through technical and 
management materials, training, and expert advice!
