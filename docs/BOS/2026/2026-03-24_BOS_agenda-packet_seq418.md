CALL TO THE PUBLIC: 
Chairman Seymore explained that the Call to the Public format has been revised and
will now be reserved for general comments; individuals wishing to speak on a specific
agenda item will be invited to do so when that item is presented.
Donna Shurwin greeted the board, stating that she lives in Pinetop, Arizona. She noted
that she had spoken at the February 10, 2026, meeting to request that an agenda item
regarding Real AZ be added and expressed disappointment that her request had not
been honored. Ms. Shurwin stated that concerns are increasing among Navajo County
constituents regarding the county’s involvement with Real AZ, and that many citizens
question why Navajo County continues to engage with an entity they believe to be
dissolved. She further questioned whether it is legal for the private entity known as Real
AZ to operate as Navajo County’s formal economic development department with
shared employees and office space within the government complex.  Ms. Shurwin
expressed her view that Real AZ only pursues the green energy industry, which she
believes does not foster balanced economic growth and instead negatively affects the
County’s longstanding tourism industry. She questioned the fairness of Real AZ’s
practices, stating that they appear to benefit paying members rather than serve the
interests of Navajo County residents. Ms. Shurwin stated her belief that Real AZ aligns
itself with entities seeking green energy subsidies and tax incentives, claiming that
Navajo County citizens will bear the costs through taxes, higher energy bills, and
diminished economic opportunities.  She further stated that the existing solar farms and
wind turbines have consumed large tracts of land, disrupted sacred sites, affected
wildlife habitats, and eradicated agricultural industry.  She expressed the view that green
energy projects provide temporary jobs, reduce property values, deflate legacies,
contribute to unemployment and do not address rising living costs or provide lasting
benefits.  Ms. Shurwin concluded by requesting that the Board place her proposed
agenda items on the March 24, 2026, agenda: to impose an immediate moratorium on
all Navajo County wind, solar, and battery storage projects to allow time for further
evaluation of their impacts, and to disassociate from and dissolve Real AZ in order to
restore fairness to the County’s energy policy.
Chairman Seymore stated that an item will be placed on a future agenda to address
Ms. Shurwin’s concerns.

Kelly Meixler thanked the Board for allowing her to speak and introduced herself as a
local business owner committed to safeguarding Navajo County’s future.  She stated
that she is also asking the Board to place Ms. Shurwin’s suggested items on a future
agenda.  Ms. Meixler said she’d build on the moratorium that Ms. Shurwin asked for,
outlining several actionable steps that she believes will protect the grid, property values,
and the people of Navajo County. Ms. Meixler said the first step she suggests is to
rescind Resolution 17-2025, stating that the measure influenced by Real AZ has
enabled unchecked renewable expansion without protection, leading to grid instability
and lower property values. She explained that studies show homes near industrial solar
sites lose 10-20% in value, explaining that rescinding Resolution 17-2025 will clear the
way for policies centered on reliability while addressing privacy concerns regarding flock
cameras that SB111 and Representative Marshall’s bill curb to aim.  She expressed her
view that the County should fight for a small nuclear reactor at the Cholla Power Plant,
claiming that its existing infrastructure, transmission lines, water access, and workforce
base make it ideal for a thorium-based SNR offering advancements in safe, efficient
nuclear technology with minimal waste and enhanced safety features, which could spark
a boom in Arizona mining, revitalizing thorium extraction and creating a new industrial
hub.  She expressed her view that an SNR would generate hundreds of permanent jobs
for locals, would boost the tax base and align with Federal Department of Energy
initiatives, potentially securing 225 to 625 million dollars in modernization funds and
ensuring safe, secure power without foreign dependencies. Ms. Meixler reaffirmed that a
comprehensive plan would include robust ordinances, zoning laws, mandatory
environmental and community impact assessments, two-mile setbacks from homes,
requiring voter approval for large scale projects, implementing grid safeguards
mandating 99% reliability, and creating a local energy fund from developer fees to
support worker transitions and offset rate increases.  Ms. Meixler concluded by stating
that the Board should enact a moratorium on renewables, dissolve Real AZ, rescind
resolution 17-2025, fight for an SNR at Cholla, and enact protective ordinances. She
then presented the Board with some articles that supported her suggestions.
Joan Lewis addressed the Board, stating that she is from Snowflake and a longtime
resident of Navajo County. She asked the Board to revisit Resolution 17?2025 and the
June 2025 zoning updates, which she stated weakened turbine setbacks and eased
approvals for renewable energy projects. Ms. Lewis noted that while some conditions
were added, she believes the changes prioritized intermittent energy projects over rural
character and community needs. Ms. Lewis stated that residents requesting stronger
protections, such as larger buffers, were denied, and she expressed the view that the
final vote was rushed with limited outreach. She referenced the Arizona Corporation
Commission’s recent approval of gas conversions at the Springerville and Coronado
plants, stating that these conversions will provide reliable baseload power, reduce
emissions without requiring batteries, prevent blackouts, and preserve jobs. She shared
her belief that transmission lines should carry firm power, gas now and nuclear power in
the future. Ms. Lewis urged the Board to impose an immediate moratorium on all
renewable energy projects, including solar, wind, and battery storage, to halt what she
described as ongoing damage and to assess the true costs. She referenced SB111
and Representative Marshall’s bill and stated that converting the Cholla plant to gas
would result in less disruption and faster power delivery. Ms. Lewis also requested
larger buffers, suggesting 10 miles. She concluded by asking the Board to cut ties with
Real AZ, pullback Resolution 17-2025 and to prioritize people over permits.

b. Budget presentation for fiscal year 2026-2027 General Fund revenue
projections. 
A presentation was shown
Jayson Vowell addressed the Board and stated that he would be presenting
the first step in preparing the Fiscal Year 2027 budget, focusing on revenues,
forecasting, and projections. He explained that the general fund functions as a
“melting pot” from which many services are provided. Mr. Vowell reported that
revenues remain strong and robust, noting that they have exceeded
projections over the past several years. For the current fiscal year, his team is
projecting a positive variance of approximately $2.4 million. Mr. Vowell further
explained that, historically, about 79% of the revenue in the general fund is
collected from taxes.  He explained that the 5-year average increase in state
sales tax revenue has been 7.3% and the 5-year average increase in county
sales tax revenue has been about 7.5%. He explained that his team uses
conservative projections to safeguard against potential recessionary impacts
and statewide economic pressures, including inflation, housing affordability,
savings trends, and fluctuations in retail spending, particularly from remote
purchasing. Mr. Vowell then said that the County is proposing to keep the
current property tax rate in fiscal year 2027.  Mr. Vowell summarized that the
increase from fiscal year 26 to 27 is an increase of approximately 6.7% which
is subject to change. However, his team is not expecting any major impacts.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger expressed gratitude for the work done by the
finance department to maintain a balanced budget.  She then stated that as a
property owner she is appreciative of the flat property tax rate year after year
and that she appreciates the department’s efforts in helping to support
community members by continuing to project the flat property tax rate.
Chairman Seymore stated that many issues arise at the state level that
supervisors must work to keep within state budgets rather than shifting to
county budgets. He noted that the state has still not addressed the future of the
“Big Beautiful Bill” or how it will impact the state budget this year. Chairman
Seymore expressed his appreciation for the finance team’s efforts in staying
informed about developments at the national, state, and county levels when
preparing financial projections.

a. Presentation by the Family Advocacy Center
A presentation was shown
Catherine Doyle introduced herself as the Director of the Navajo County Family
Advocacy Center, a 501c3 that operates under the Navajo County Attorney’s
Office.  Ms. Doyle explained that the center is designed to help children and
their families, that the center was created by the County Attorney’s office to fill
a gap of services in the community, and that it is a non-profit organization
based off donations from the community.  Ms. Doyle said that her presentation
uses statistics from Navajo and Apache counties explaining that every 36
hours a child crime is committed and that 99% of the victims know their
offender and that year over year there is a continued increase in victims. Ms.
Doyle shared that the Family Advocacy Center's mission statement is that the
“Navajo County Family Advocacy Center’s goal is to reduce the stress that
families, especially children, may face as victims of abuse, neglect, domestic
violence, or sexual assault during the investigation and prosecution of a crime
against them.”  Ms. Doyle stated that services have continually been added

WHAT EVERYONE SHOULD KNOW ABOUT 
FAIR HOUSING 
The sale and purchase of a home is one of the most 
significant events that an individual will experience  
in their lifetime. It is more than the simple purchase 
of housing, for it directly impacts the hopes, dreams, 
aspirations, and economic destiny of those involved. 
It is for this reason that the Fair Housing Act and other 
federal and state laws were enacted to guarantee 
a right to a national housing market free from 
discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, 
disability, familial status, and national origin. 
THE LAW 
Civil Rights Act of 1866 – The Civil Rights Act of 1866 
prohibits all racial discrimination in the sale or rental of 
property. 
 
Fair Housing Act – With the enactment of the US Civil 
Rights Act of 1968, fair housing rights became federal 
law. Title VIII of the Act, as amended by Congress in 
1988 prohibits discrimination based upon the race, 
color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national 
origin of those seeking housing. 
 
In Arizona, state fair housing laws are equivalent  
to federal law. In some communities, local housing 
ordinances make it illegal to deny housing based on   
age, marital status, or sexual orientation. These fair 
housing laws protect the right of each home seeker and 
provides equal opportunity in the purchase, sale, rental, 
leasing, financing, insuring, and advertising of housing. 
WHAT HOUSING IS COVERED 
The Fair Housing Act covers most housing. In some 
circumstances, the Act exempts owner-occupied 
buildings with no more than four units, single -family 
housing sold or rented without the use of a broker, and 
housing operated by organizations and private clubs 
that limit occupancy to members. 
HOUSING DISCRIMINATION STILL EXISTS 
Despite a wide range of housing opportunities 
throughout Arizona, the doors of homes, apartments, 
mobile homes, and condominiums are closed to 
many because of illegal discrimination. Complaints to 
and testing by fair housing organizations in Arizona 
indicate that discrimination is a common practice, 
frequently undetected by home seekers who are 
unlawfully denied access to housing. You have the right 
to expect that housing will be available to you without 
discrimination or other limitations based on race, color, 
religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national origin. 
SIGNS OF HOUSING DISCRIMINATION 
 Refusing to sell, rent, or show available housing. 
 Only showing housing in areas where other 
minorities live. 
 Harassment or intimidation. 
 Housing advertisements with discriminatory 
statements or displaying no minorities in group 
scenes. 
 Differing terms for identical dwellings. 
 Extensive questioning prior to offering or 
providing information about the availability of 
housing. 
 Being told the dwellings is not appropriate for your 
family. 
 Terms of availability change between phone 
contact and your visit. 
 You are not contacted after the acceptance of your 
application. 
 House or apartment has an “available” sign but you 
are told it is not available. 
 Refusing to make reasonable accommodation 
or allow a modification to make the dwelling 
accessible for a person with a disability. 
 Refusing to finance the purchase of a home or to 
write property insurance, or offering non-standard 
and unfavorable terms. 
SECTION 504 
Section 504 prohibits discrimination on  the  basis 
of disability in any program or activity that receives 
financial assistance from any federal agency. 
 
With respect to housing, a housing provider may not: 
 Deny or refuse to sell or rent to a person with a 
disability. 
 Impose application or qualification criteria that 
is different than those required of or provided to 
persons who are not disable. 
 Impose rental fees or sales prices, and rental or sale 
terms or conditions that are different than those 
required of or provided to persons who are not 
disable. 
 Require persons with disabilities to live only on 
certain floors 
 Deny those with disabilities access to recreational 
or other public and common use facilities. 
 Charge a higher security deposit to a person that 
uses a wheelchair 
AMERICANS WITH DISABILITIES ACT (ADA) 
Title II 
Covers activities of public entities (state and local 
governments). It requires public entities to make 
both new and existing housing facilities accessible 
to persons with disabilities. Housing covered by Title 
II of the ADA includes, for example, public housing 
authorities that meet the ADA definition of “public 
entity,” and housing operated by States or local 
government, such as housing on a state university 
campus. 
 
Title III 
Requires that public and common use areas at housing 
developments are accessible.

LO QUE TODO EL MUNDO DEBE SABER ACERCA DE 
LA JUSTÍCIA EN VIVIENDAS 
La venta y compra de una casa es uno de los eventos más 
significativos  que un individuo experimentará  durante 
su vida. Es más que una simple compra de una vivienda, 
porque impacta directamente las esperanzas, los sueños, 
las aspiraciones  y el destino económico de los involucrados. 
Es por esta razón que el Acto de Justicia en Viviendas y 
otras leyes federales y estatales fueron promulgados para 
garantizar el derecho a un mercado de vivienda nacional 
libre de discriminación  basada en raza, color, religión, sexo, 
incapacidad, estado familiar y origen nacional. 
 
LA LEY 
 
Civil Rights Act of 1866 —The Civil Rights Act of 1866 (El Acto 
de Derechos Civiles de 1866) prohíbe toda discriminación 
racial en la venta o alquiler de propiedad. 
 
Acto de Justicia en Viviendas —  Con la promulgación del 
US Acto de Derechos Civiles de 1968, derecho de justicia en 
viviendas pasó a ser ley federal. Título VIII del Acto, como 
enmendado por el Congreso en 1988 prohíbe discriminación 
basada en raza, color, religión, sexo, estado familiar, o origen 
nacional de los que buscan viviendas. 
 
En Arizona, las leyes de justicia en viviendas son equivalentes 
a la ley federal. En algunas comunidades, las ordenanzas 
locales de viviendas hacen ilegal la negación de vivienda 
basada en la edad, estado matrimonial  o orientación sexual. 
Estas leyes justas de vivienda protegen el derecho de cada 
buscador de vivienda y proporciona oportunidad igual en 
la compra, venta, alquiler, arrendamiento, financiación y 
anuncios de viviendas. 
CUALES VIVIENDAS ESTÁN CUBIERTAS  
 
El Acto de Justicia en Viviendas cubre casi todas las viviendas. 
En algunas circunstancias,  el Acto exenta edificios ocupados 
por el dueño con no más de cuatro unidades, viviendas de 
una sola familia vendida o alquilada sin el uso de un agente, y 
viviendas operadas por organizaciones  y clubes privados que 
limitan ocupación a los  miembros. 
DISCRIMINACIÓN EN VIVIENDAS TODAVÍA EXISTE  
A pesar de la amplia escala de oportunidades por toda 
Arizona, las puertas de casas, apartamentos,  casas móviles, y 
condominios  están cerradas para muchas personas por causa 
de la discriminación ilegal. Quejas par a y las pruebas por 
organizaciones de justicia en viviendas en Arizona indican 
que la discriminación  es una práctica común, frecuentemente 
no descubierta por los que buscan viviendas que son 
negados ilegalmente acceso a viviendas. Usted tiene el 
derecho de esperar que viviendas sean disponibles para si sin 
discriminación  u otra limitación basada en raza, color, religión, 
sexo, incapacidad,  estado familiar, u origen nacional. 
SEÑALES DE DISCRIMINACIÓN DE VIVIENDAS  
 
 Rehusar a vender, alquilar o mostrar viviendas 
disponibles. 
 Solamente mostrar viviendas en áreas donde otras 
minoridades viven. 
 Acoso o intimidación.  
 Anuncios de viviendas con declaraciones  discriminatorias 
o exhibiendo escenas de grupos sin minoridades.  
 Diferentes condiciones para viviendas idénticas. 
 Preguntas extensivas previo a ofrecer o fornecer 
información sobre la disponibilidad de viviendas. 
 Siendo informado que la vivienda no es apropiada para 
su familia. 
 Términos de disponibilidad cambian entre su contacto 
por teléfono y su  visita. 
 Usted no sea contactado después de la aceptación de su 
solicitud. 
 La casa o el apartamento tiene un aviso de 
“disponibilidad”  pero usted es avisado que no es 
disponible. 
 Rehusando a hacer una acomodación razonable o 
permitir una modificación  para una persona incapacitada.  
 Rehusando a financiar la compra de una casa o a 
extender seguro de propiedad, o la oferta de términos 
que no sean normales o desfavorables.  
SECCIÓN 504 
La Sección 504 prohíbe discriminación en la base de 
incapacidad en cualquier programa o actividad que recibe 
auxilio financiero de cualquier agencia federal. 
 
Con respecto a viviendas, un proveedor de viviendas no 
puede: 
 
 Negar o rehusar a vender o alquilar para una persona con 
una incapacidad.  
 Imponer un criterio de solicitación  o calificación que sea 
diferente al que se requiere o es fornecido a personas que 
no sean incapacitadas.  
 Imponer cuotas de alquiler o precios de venta y términos 
o condiciones  de alquiler o venta que sean diferentes de 
los requeridos de o provistos para personas que no sean 
incapacitadas.  
 Exigir personas incapacitadas a vivir solamente en ciertos 
pisos. 
 Negar a las personas incapacitadas  acceso a los servicios 
de recreo u otro uso público y común. 
 Cobrar un depósito de seguro más alto para la persona 
que use una silla de  ruedas. 
 
AMERICANS WITH DISABILITIES ACT (ADA) 
(ACTO DE AMERICANOS CON INCAPACIDADES) 
 
Title II 
Cubre las actividades de entidades (gobiernos estatales 
e locales). Requiere que las entidades públicas hagan 
accesibles las instalaciones  de vivienda, tanto nuevas como ya 
existentes, a personas con incapacidad. Viviendas cubiertas 
por el Título II del ADA incluyen, por ejemplo, autoridades  
de viviendas públicas que llenen la definición de “entidad 
pública” del ADA y viviendas operadas por los Estados o 
gobierno local, tal como viviendas en el campus de una 
universidad estatal. 
 
Title III 
Requiere que las áreas públicas y de uso común en 
urbanizaciones sean accesibles.

USDA Forest Service News Release
The U.S. Forest Service today announced it is issuing
$182 million in retroactive Secure Rural Schools (SRS) payments for 2024, delivering critical

sustain public schools, maintain local roads, strengthen wildﬁre preparedness, and support
other essential services in rural communities.
“For many rural counties, SRS payments aren’t optional – they’re essential,” said Forest Service
Chief Tom Schultz. “The Forest Service’s annual payments provide reliable, predictable funding
that rural states and counties depend on. These payments strengthen local schools and
infrastructure while honoring the partnership between national forests and the communities
that surround them.”
The 2024 payments include Title I and Title III allocations, which help fund education,
transportation infrastructure and critical community services in rural areas. They also enhance
wildﬁre readiness at the local level by supporting Firewise Communities programs, reimbursing
counties for emergency services on national forests, and supporting the development of
Community Wildﬁre Protection Plans — making communities safer and improving coordination
with Forest Service wildﬁre response efforts.
Because SRS had not yet been reauthorized when 2024 payments were issued, the Forest
Service initially made payments under the 1908 revenue-sharing framework, as required by law.
With reauthorization, the agency is now reconciling payments to ensure counties receive the

2.11 EMPLOYEE SERVICE RECOGNITION AND RETENTION
2.111 Policy:
Employee retention is an operational necessity for Navajo County and a practical,
cost-effective use of public resources. Replacing employees requires significant spending
on recruitment, onboarding, and training, as well as lost productivity and increased
workload for remaining staff. In contrast, recognizing and supporting long-serving
employees helps preserve institutional knowledge, maintain service quality, and reduce
the financial and operational impacts of turnover. The Service Recognition and Retention
Program is therefore a core workforce strategy designed to acknowledge all employees’
length of service, reinforce their value to the organization, and promote continued
commitment to Navajo County. Within this context, the County retains the exclusive right to
determine the most appropriate manner to administer service recognition, and
presentation methods may vary. This may include hosting occasional employee recognition
events or gatherings throughout the year to formally acknowledge collective achievements,
strengthen relationships among employees and leadership, reinforce a culture of
appreciation and engagement, increase employees’ sense of belonging, and connection to
the County’s mission, which can reduce turnover and sustain a stable, committed
workforce. The underlying objective is to retain workforce talent through consistent
recognition and support of employees and their commitment to Navajo County. The County
has observed positive outcomes from these and similar strategies through data collection
and reporting, demonstrating a clear business case for recognizing employees and their
dedication to Navajo County.
The following broad guidelines shall apply to the Service Recognition and Retention
Program:
A. The County will recognize employees at five-year intervals of continuous or aggregate
qualifying service (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, etc.) and at retirement.
B. Recognition may include a commemorative certificate, item(s), emblem/pin, and/or
recognition leave, subject to budget availability and applicable laws. Items given will
be di minimis in value per the IRS guidelines.
C. The County may hold recognition events and may combine service awards with other
recognition ceremonies, provided activities are reasonable in duration and
conducted during work hours when possible.
D. Recognition is honorary and does not alter employment status, classification,
seniority for layoff, or pay.

<!-- page 73 -->
E. The County may host occasional employee events or gatherings throughout the year.
These events may include food and refreshments, team building exercises, item(s),
and informal opportunities for employees to connect with leadership, colleagues,
and family members to promote team cohesion and morale. Events will be planned
in a cost-effective manner and may be scheduled to coincide with seasonal activities
or other appropriate occasions. To support participation by employees working
varied schedules, the County will make reasonable efforts to hold events at different
times and days throughout the year. Participation in recognition events is open to all
employees, and supervisors will make reasonable efforts to allow employee
attendance when operational needs permit.
2.112 Coverage:
This policy applies to all classified and unclassified positions. Nothing in this policy
modifies or waives the “at will” status of an unclassified employee.
2.113 Eligibility
This policy applies to all full-time and part-time permanent employees. Years of service will
be calculated off of your most recent hire date.
2.114 Exceptions to Policy

Forest Service Announces $182 Million in Retroactive Secure Rural Schools Payments to Support Counties,
Schools, and Wildﬁre Preparedness
(Washington, D.C., February 20, 2026 ) -
Forest Service Announces $182 Million in
Retroactive Secure Rural Schools Payments to
Support Counties, Schools, and Wildﬁre
Preparedness
USDA Forest Service News Release
The U.S. Forest Service today announced it is issuing
$182 million in retroactive Secure Rural Schools (SRS) payments for 2024, delivering critical
funding to eligible states and counties with National Forest System lands. These payments help
sustain public schools, maintain local roads, strengthen wildﬁre preparedness, and support
other essential services in rural communities.
“For many rural counties, SRS payments aren’t optional – they’re essential,” said Forest Service
Chief Tom Schultz. “The Forest Service’s annual payments provide reliable, predictable funding
that rural states and counties depend on. These payments strengthen local schools and
infrastructure while honoring the partnership between national forests and the communities
that surround them.”
The 2024 payments include Title I and Title III allocations, which help fund education,
transportation infrastructure and critical community services in rural areas. They also enhance
wildﬁre readiness at the local level by supporting Firewise Communities programs, reimbursing
counties for emergency services on national forests, and supporting the development of
Community Wildﬁre Protection Plans — making communities safer and improving coordination
with Forest Service wildﬁre response efforts.
Because SRS had not yet been reauthorized when 2024 payments were issued, the Forest
Service initially made payments under the 1908 revenue-sharing framework, as required by law.
With reauthorization, the agency is now reconciling payments to ensure counties receive the
full amounts owed under the program.

Since 1908, 25% of Forest Service revenue from timber sales, mineral leases, livestock grazing,
recreation fees and other sources have been shared with states and counties containing
national forests. By the 1990s, long-term reductions in timber revenue signiﬁcantly reduced
these payments, prompting Congress to enact the Secure Rural Schools and Community Self-
Determination Act of 2000 to provide more stable funding for at-risk local services.
Over the past decade, the Forest Service has distributed $2.6 billion through the SRS program.

Department: NC Public Health Services District Division: Emergency Management
Motion before the Board:
Approval of the Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) between Navajo County Public Health District
and the City of Show Low for the deployment of closed Point of Dispensing (POD)
Background:
This item requests approval of a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) between the Navajo County
Public Health Services District and the City of Show Low to establish a Closed Point of Dispensing
(POD) during public health emergencies. Under this agreement, the Navajo County Public Health
Services District will provide medical countermeasures—such as vaccines, medications or other
supplies—from the Strategic National Stockpile to the City of Show Low for distribution to designated
city employees and essential personnel during a declared emergency.
The purpose of the agreement is to strengthen local emergency preparedness and ensure the timely
protection of essential government personnel needed to maintain critical services for the community. The
Navajo County Public Health Services District will provide guidance, coordination, and training support
for the operation of the closed POD, while the City of Show Low will be responsible for staffing and
internal distribution to its eligible personnel.
Approval of this MOU supports regional public health preparedness and enhances coordinated response
capabilities between the county and municipal partners. No fiscal impact to Navajo County is anticipated.

I. Purpose 
This Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) is made and entered into by and between 
Navajo County Public Health District and the City of Show Low (hereinafter “Provider”) 
for the purpose of cooperation as it relates to the deployment and dispensing of 
prophylactic medications and other supplies made available from the Centers for Disease 
Control and Prevention’s (CDC) Strategic National Stockpile (SNS), through the Navajo 
County Public Health District. 
 
WHEREAS,  Navajo County Public Health District will receive emergency prophylactic 
medications through the CDC SNS program following a widespread bio-event or 
epidemics, and Navajo County Public Health District will be responsible for delivering 
the same to potentially exposed populations in a highly efficient manner to save lives; 
and, 
 
WHEREAS, Navajo County Public Health District seeks a mechanism to provide these 
prophylactic medications to potentially exposed populations that may be required to 
report to duty and unable to pick up at public Points of Dispensing (PODs); and,  
 
WHEREAS, the Provider seeks to safeguard its emergency response staff, and has the 
infrastructure and resources to dispense medical prophylaxis to these populations upon 
receipt of medicine; and, 
 
WHEREAS,  Navajo County Public Health District and the Provider intend to cooperate 
and collaborate to serve the community in such an event,

WHEREAS, the week of April 6, 2026, is National Public Health Week and the theme is “Ready. Set. Action!” highlighting that
public health actions occurring through scientific discoveries, legislation and community initiatives have significantly improved the
health of all Americans;
WHEREAS, significant strides have been made in public health since the inception of National Public Health Week in 1994,
including the following public health successes:
(1) childhood mortality, which was at a rate of 13.8 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1994, has now fallen to 5.4 deaths per 1,000 live
births due to vaccines, better health care access and nutrition;
(2) smoking rates, which were as high as 25.5% among U.S. adults in 1994, have now fallen to 11.4 % due to comprehensive smoke-
free policies, public awareness campaigns and taxation of tobacco products;
(3) deaths due to HIV/AIDS, which peaked in 1994 at 49,095 AIDS-related deaths that year in the U.S., have dropped significantly
with Antiretroviral therapy ensuring most people with HIV can live normal lifespans and prevention tools like PrEP significantly
reduce transmission;
(4) mental health awareness and treatment, with 40% of people with mental health conditions being untreated in 1994, has progressed
with increased awareness, parity laws and integration into primary care, which have expanded access to mental health services and
reduced treatment gaps;
(5) lead poisoning, with 4.4% of U.S. children having elevated blood lead levels in 1994, has now fallen to less than 0.5% due to
policies banning leaded gasoline and reducing lead in paint, pipes and water systems;
(6) health impacts of climate change such as rising heat-related deaths were underrecognized before the new millennium but are now
being mitigated by actions such as public health campaigns, local adaptation plans including cooling centers and other preparedness
plans to protect the public from extreme weather, wildfires and other regional threats;
(7) pandemic preparedness has improved rapidly, especially led by scientific advancement including mRNA technology and global
vaccine deployment, and communities have increased investments in infrastructure, stockpiles and global surveillance — all of which
must be sustained.
WHEREAS, racial and ethnic minority populations in the United States continue to experience disparities in the burden of illness and
death as compared with the entire population of the United States;
WHEREAS, a person’s health status can differ drastically by ZIP code due to differences in the built environment, environmental
quality, community context, access to healthy food, access to education and access to health care;
WHEREAS, public health organizations use National Public Health Week to educate public policymakers, public health professionals
and the public on issues that are important to improving the health of the people of the United States;
WHEREAS, public health professionals help communities prevent, prepare for, withstand and recover from the impact of a full range
of health threats, ranging from infectious disease outbreaks to natural disaster preparedness; and
WHEREAS, efforts to adequately support public health and the prevention of disease and injury can continue to transform a health
system focused on treating illness into a health system focused on preventing disease and injury and promoting wellness.
WHEREAS, The Board of Supervisors would like to officially recognize the hard work and dedication of all those involved in
improving the health status of individuals and communities, through public health initiatives, interventions, education and support;
including healthcare providers, public health workers, and most of all, the citizens themselves for accepting responsibility for their

Motion before the Board:
Consideration of an Intergovernmental Agreement between the Navajo County Flood Control District and
the Silver Creek Flood Control District for financial assistance for the Silver Creek Drainage Maintenance
and Improvements Projects in the amount of $50,000.00 per year for two consecutive years.
Background:
The Silver Creek Flood Control District (SCFCD) is requesting financial assistance with their
maintenance of the Silver Creek drainage area. The purpose of this IGA is to authorize the County to
partner financially, as the Navajo County Flood Control District, with the SCFCD. We share a common
interest in improving the Silver Creek drainage area to help reduce the risk of future flooding. The
SCFCD requests financial assistance from the Navajo County Flood Control District to reimburse the
SCFD based on paid invoices for maintenance costs within the Silver Creek drainage area. Payment to the
District will be consistent with progress and completion of the ongoing maintenance requirement. The
SCFCD shall be solely responsible for the maintenance. The SCFCD understands and agrees that the
County's sole participation and obligation with respect to the maintenance shall be to provide financial
assistance in the amount not to exceed $50,000.00 per year for two consecutive years. Public Works has
reviewed the request and finds it consistent with the purpose of the Navajo County Flood Control District
and supports the Project.
Recommendation: 
Staff recommends approval of this proposed IGA.
