3.

Presentation to introduce the 2026 Navajo County Community Health Needs Assessment Survey (CHNAS).
Ciera Hensley, Deputy Health Director for Navajo County, greeted the Board, expressing her excitement to share information about the upcoming Community Health Needs Assessment Survey (CHNAS) that will be launching on February 2, 2026. Ciera explained that the survey gives residents a direct voice in health needs and barriers, and priorities across the county. The survey ensures that the county's planning and investments are grounded in lived experience and not assumptions. Ciera said that the survey is a community-wide survey that is done every 3 years and is made available to Navajo County residents, 18 and older, online or on paper. The survey will be available from February 2, 2026, through April 30, 2026, takes 15 minutes to complete, and will cover a variety of health topics including physical health, social health, mental health, access to healthcare, cost of healthcare, substance use, and social determinants of health.  She said that the survey is accessible and designed to provide meaningful data. The survey follows a framework called the MAPP 2.0 Framework, which stands for Mobilizing for Action through Planning and Partnerships and includes 3 phases that are important to the process.  Ciera provided a recap of the last assessment launched in February 2023.  With the survey data, Navajo County Public Health identified community health priorities such as STI, social determinant of health, chronic diseases and mental health, and substance use.  By September 2023, they were able to publish their community health assessment report to the public and later release the community health improvement plan. She then stated that at the beginning of 2024 they were able to launch work groups focused on the health priorities of the community. She explained that the data from the survey is important to Navajo County and Public Health because it strengthens funding and partnerships, strong community input improves program outcomes, helps in funding applications to align with partners, and supports our local hospitals.
Next, Ciera focused on how the survey would be advertised, stating that she and the Health Project Services Manager, Sherry Fish, would be traveling throughout the county.  She thanked their community partners, Summit Healthcare, Little Colorado Medical Center, and Changepoint for their collaboration and Alicia Deets for sharing information on Navajo County's social media platforms. Ciera highlighted two competitions, a photo competition where community members can submit landscape photos of Navajo County; the winning photos will be added to the final reports and a competition between the towns and cities of Navajo County to see which can get the highest number of surveys submitted. Ciera asked the board to consider sharing information about the survey in their networks as well.
Supervisor Whiting thanked the Navajo County Public Health team for the presentation and recognized that the survey is important and takes a lot of coordination to get the community involved to have a meaningful assessment.

BOARD OF SUPERVISORS:

a.

Discussion and possible action regarding a Development Agreement with SE Cygnus, LLC for the development and operation of an up-to 650-megawatt solar energy facility in Navajo County with.  Pursuant to A.R.S. §38-431.03(A)(3) the Board may convene in Executive Session to receive legal advice from its attorney.
Chairman Seymore welcomed County Manager Bryan Layton to the podium, noting that after the presentation, the board may move into Executive Session to receive legal advice from their attorney.
County Manager Bryan Layton said he would start the presentation with context and, with the permission of the Board, would invite Planning Manager Cody Cooper to share some technical details about the project. Mr. Layton started by answering the question of why renewable energy products are interested in Navajo County, explaining that Navajo County does not go out and seek these projects. Mr. Layton said that Navajo County is a great place with abundant wind and solar resources, existing infrastructure and transmission lines in place, landowners that are willing to participate, favorable topography, and the state provides an 80% reduction in property tax. Mr. Layton shared a graphic illustrating that counties are required to have a comprehensive plan which is the basis of everything that is done in planning, zoning, and building; built upon the comprehensive plan is zoning and corridor plans, site plans, building plans, and then receiving a certificate of occupancy. Mr. Layton explained that the process of updating the comprehensive plan was started 2 years ago as required by state statute. He stated that as they started that process, a review of ordinances was triggered.  He said that the Board asked staff to start with the renewable energy ordinance first and that it has now been updated and completed.  Bryan explained that state statute requires the county to perform certain types of public outreach when updating the comprehensive plan.  He shared a slide detailing where, when and how the county adhered to that and said that beyond what is required by statute, the county also held community meetings, conducted interviews, and provided digital tools for residents to provide feedback and their perspective on the process. He said that the county also held 3 listening sessions in Show Low, Heber, and Joseph City to hear directly from Navajo County residents. Mr. Layton stated that from all the input they were able to create a framework for evaluating renewable projects.  The first step in the review is to evaluate whether the agreement meets or exceeds all the criterion standards required by the county ordinance and if so, staff will move on to looking at the preferred criteria. Mr. Layton explained that the public's preferred criteria focused on minimizing visual impact, appropriate location, use of previously disturbed land with low habitat value or limited vegetation, and water conservation. He added that the projects should also provide a community benefit, noting that these criteria formed the framework used to evaluate the project.  Mr. Layton then invited Cody Cooper to provide some technical details and an overview of the project itself.
Cody Cooper greeted the Board and proceeded to go over the staff review that was completed as part of the submittal for the development agreement that Planning and Development Services conducted for the components that had been proposed with the Cygnus Solar Project. Mr. Cooper highlighted the proposal that includes up to 650 megawatts of photo photovoltaic solar energy and a 400-megawatt capacity battery energy storage system with a 200-foot generation tie-in line, located on 3,721 acres located 10 miles north of Snowflake, AZ. Mr. Cooper then went over the components of the review starting with the design, stating that it met or exceeded all standards that exist in the Navajo County Zoning Ordinance.  Next, he went over the signage, stating that the setback standards have been met or exceeded.  Mr. Cooper stated that the fencing was an important component to the public, and that perimeter fencing would incorporate wildlife movement corridors and would include six-to-eight-inch gaps at the bottom for small wildlife movement as recommended by the Arizona Game and Fish Department. Mr. Cooper explained that the developer has agreed to preserve dark sky characteristics using shielded motion-activated light fixtures and no strobe lighting will be employed unless specifically required by the FAA. He noted that Navajo County is not a Dark Sky Community as far as ordinances go, so the lighting exceeds the ordinance. Mr. Cooper went on to review the road use and setbacks, stating that they have been reviewed to make sure they will not cause any major impact on the neighboring properties.  Mr. Copper then explained that bonds to cover reclamation and restoration will be required prior to construction and updated every 5 years throughout the duration of the project if accepted and that the developer has agreed to go to 48 inches below ground upon decommissioning, which exceeds the industry standard and the zoning ordinance, which is 3 feet.
Supervisor Whiting asked Mr. Cooper to clarify how the bonding would work to make sure that if the developer were to become insolvent, the taxpayers would not have to absorb the cost.
Cody Cooper stated that if the project were to be purchased by another entity or if it were to be taken over by a financier, the bonding is structured so that any financial requirements of the original developer will have to be transferred to the new entity, reiterating that there are contingencies put in place that will require that bonding to remain throughout the life of the project, regardless of who is in control of the project or property.
Supervisor Whiting asked Cody to explain in simple terms for the public why that is an acceptable solution and what bonding entails.
Cody Cooper stated that the bonding will require the amount of money that has been put together by a professional engineer to decommission the project and restore it back to the natural state will be held for the duration of the project.  Mr. Cooper explained that bonding is a way to hold money for the entirety of the project, regardless of entity, to make sure that there is not anything left behind with the project and no cost incurred by taxpayers at a later date.
Mr. Cooper explained that the next step in the review process was to review the preferred criteria that the Board wanted to employ for the project. He said that one of the preferred criteria was visual impact and finding ways to minimize this. Mr. Cooper showed a map and explained that from the nearest public roadways looking at the project you cannot see the project because of the topography, that there are hills and other features that block the view of the project from the public roadways.  Mr. Cooper went over an additional preferred criterion that the project be cited near existing substations and transmission lines.  He stated that this project is right next to the transmission lines and the existing Sugarloaf Substation.  He then reviewed the preferred water conservation criteria, stating that the developer has agreed to limit the washing of the panels to twice a year.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger asked about the conservation methods for water usage and asked if there would be water transported to the site.
Mr. Cooper replied that the applicant can transport water to the site or use any entitlements on the site that come through the Department of Water Resources, stating that while in construction it is typical for these projects to go offsite and fill some water trucks to help with dust control on site.
County Manager Bryan Layton then went over the community benefits of the project.  He recapped a property tax breakout in Navajo County showing that 47% of each tax dollar goes to local school districts, 23% to the fire district, 16% to the Northland Pioneer College District, 8% to the county, and 6% to secondary districts like flood control, library district, and public health. Mr. Layton referred to the 80% tax break incentives from the state for these types of projects.  He then stated that the project would last for 35 years and that the Assessor has worked with the developer and the estimated, but subject to change, amount of funding over 35 years would be, to the Snowflake and Holbrook school districts 20 million dollars, Northland Pioneer College 5.5 million, the Navajo County general fund 2.2 million, the Flood Control District $783k, the Public Health District $718k, the Library District $286k, and NAVIT $158k.  He then showed a slide that explains how, over time, the developer is allowed to depreciate the value of the assets on the property and the corresponding property tax will go down. Mr. Layton went over the community impacts, such as the influx of temporary workers who will provide increased consumerism to support local business but may place strain on housing code enforcement, public safety infrastructure, and county finances.  He stated that to combat that concern, the developer has agreed to provide Navajo County with a one-time 6-million-dollar contribution, termed a community benefit. Mr. Layton explained that the first $250,000 would be paid within the first 30 days to the county to start recruiting and developing personnel positions to mitigate those concerns. The remainder is to be paid within 270 days, and the Board of Supervisors will review where those funds are sent as part of the annual budget development process. Mr. Layton then explained that the project is not located within a fire district, and that the developer is required by ordinance to create an emergency response plan and a site-specific safety plan that is reviewed by local entities of state Fire Marshall and county staff. He said that since the properties are not located within a fire district, there is no financial contribution from property tax to the fire districts and, if approved, part of the community benefit contribution will be provided to Taylor/Snowflake Fire and Medical to support their operations.  Mr. Layton told the Board that they have been provided with a copy of the Development Agreement, which is complete with the exception of appendix E, the decommission plan which the developer needs to update in order to reconcile with the terms that have been agreed upon by the developer and planning and development services.
Chairman Seymore shared that this is not a public hearing, but that the board would allow a few comments for 3 minutes.
Buchannon Davis from Salt River Project (SRP) greeted the Board stating that his company is a proposed off taker for the project.  Mr. Davis reminded the Board and the public that SRP is a not-for-profit electric and water company in Arizona with 2 million customers. He stated that they do not have a customer base in Navajo County but that they have a significant amount of workforce at the Cornado plant in St. Johns resides in Navajo County.  He explained that there is tremendous growth in demand for utilities across the state, and they are meeting that demand with all kinds of technologies including natural gas, nuclear, and biomass. Mr. Davis said that renewable energies have come a long way and are an important tool in the toolbox, stating that solar and battery storage keeps the lights on when demand is high and reduces the reliance on costly wholesale markets. He stated that renewable energies serve as a bridge until we have future technologies, like nuclear, available.  Mr. Davis said that SRP appreciates the county's approach to the agreement that they have a genuine desire to build a partnership with Navajo County.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger asked if the the increased production of energy over the next 10 years is need-based or is it for storage.
Mr. Davis said that it is need-based due to growth in the population as well as economic growth.
Vice Chairwoman Whitesinger said that she thinks it is important for the public to understand that growth is driving the need for more power.
