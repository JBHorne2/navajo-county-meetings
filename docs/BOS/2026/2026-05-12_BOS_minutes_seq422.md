CALL TO THE PUBLIC:

Chairman Seymore opened the Call to the Public and requested that speakers avoid repeating comments already presented to the Board, whether by themselves or by previous speakers.

Liz Olmstead greeted the Board stating that she lives at 7093 Tumbleweed Trail in Linden/Show Low, AZ. Ms. Olmsted petitioned the Board on behalf of the people of Navajo County for decisive action to protect groundwater, rural life, and the integrity of county government. She expressed her view that the economic group, Real AZ had been dissolved on October 17, 2024, and is continuing to illegally operate using county staff and facilities, the county seal, and public resources.  Ms. Olmstead referred to ARS 10-3804, 10-3805, and 38-503 which she stated Real AZ is in violation of.  Ms. Olmsted expressed concerns about Real AZ donors linked to the West Camp II windfarm and perceived influence over policy changes and project approvals. Ms. Olmstead stated that their petition notes the Board’s previous enactment of Resolutions 1 2025 and 17 2025, which they believe promote renewable energy development while weakening existing protections, as well as the cancellation of all 2026 Planning and Zoning Commission meetings, which they say has limited public input on utility-scale wind proposals, particularly the proposed West Camp Wind Farm II. The petition argues that such projects would require substantial groundwater for concrete foundations and for dust suppression on new roadways in a region already experiencing aquifer stress and declining spring flows, and that no independent water-use or groundwater studies have been conducted. Ms. Olmstead further noted that the projects would qualify for an 80 percent property-tax reduction under ARS 42 14155(B), resulting in minimal long-term revenue to the County while leaving environmental and infrastructure costs unaddressed. Additional concerns she cited included potential impacts on rural landscapes, property values, wildlife habitat, and residents’ quality of life, compounded by the absence of baseline soil testing and transparent independent analysis. To address these issues, the petition requests: an immediate moratorium on all new utility-scale wind, solar, and battery-energy-storage approvals until the stated concerns are resolved; the rescission of Resolutions 1 2025 and 17 2025 in their entirety; and the severance of all ties with the dissolved Real AZ organization.

Donna Wastchak greeted the Board stating she is from Pinetop.  Ms. Wastchak addressed the Board regarding statements made during the April 28th Board meeting concerning the approval of the West Camp 2 Wind Farm Development Agreement. She stated that it was claimed the Board was required to approve the agreement and requested that the Board research that claim. Ms. Wastchak provided information on Arizona Revised Statute (ARS) 11 1101, stating that the statute gives counties discretion and authority within development agreements, including the ability to negotiate protections such as independent water-use studies, environmental studies, and requiring a Power Purchase Agreement prior to approval. She expressed concern that renewable projects could be built without being used if such safeguards were not required. Ms. Wastchak further stated that she believes Resolution 01-2025 and Resolution 17-2025, weaken county ordinances and created a fast-track approval process that removed key protections. She asked the Board to consider rescinding both resolutions and to use ARS 11 1101 as a basis for strengthening future development agreements. She also referenced ARS 11 833, stating that the statute provides the Board authority to impose an immediate moratorium on new utility-scale renewable energy projects until proper water, soil, and environmental studies are completed.

Ms. Wastchak asked the Board to listen to constituents who want stronger protections included in development agreements and urged the Board to use the statutes to act in public interest. She concluded by thanking the Board and provided copies of the statute.

Andrew Bushman introduced himself stating that he is from Joseph City and that he is working to buy back his family farm one piece at a time. He said that Joseph City and part of McLaws Road are part of a non-expansion district for irrigation dating back to the 1970’s, explaining that; that means that he cannot water more acres than what there was at the time the non-expansion district was established and that puts a cap on his business being able to expand. Mr. Bushman claimed that a data center will use hundreds and thousands of gallons of water each day is frustrating when he does not have access to water to improve his existing business.  Mr. Bushman outlined the changes to the Joseph City Irrigation District and shared his frustrations with large companies.  Mr. Bushman then turned his remaining time to Ms. Olmstead.

Liz Olmstead asked the Board to adopt the following conditions for renewable projects: two-mile setbacks from residences, springs, and wells, a signed Power Purchase Agreement, independent third-party water-use and groundwater studies, baseline soil and water testing, and robust decommissioning, bonding, and site-restoration guarantees. She also requested that the petition be placed on the agenda for the next regularly scheduled Board meeting for public hearing and consideration. Ms. Olmsted stated that residents urge the Board to prioritize the interests of Navajo County residents over outside investors.

Cheryl Piha greeted the Board and shared her concern that there is a lot of people who will get hurt if the solar and wind farms progress. She stated that she has lived in Texas, California, New York, Washington, Missouri, Florida, and now Arizona and has seen that they do not work.  She stated that she has heard about corruption and that if things keep being the same the country will be lost.  She asked the Board to remember that they serve the people who elected them and to use their power wisely.

Belinda Quog greeted the Board stating that she is from Show Low, Arizona.  Ms. Quog stated that the area is in a severe, multi-decade drought and that water supplies for millions of people, agricultural needs, and ecosystems are affected and causing extreme fire dangers. Ms. Quog stated that many wells in Navajo and Apache Counties have dried up, forcing residents to truck in water or dig deeper wells.  Ms. Quog stated that there is no water to waste on large-scale industrial projects that have no long-term benefits to the community.  Ms. Quog then referred to the state’s 80% tax break for renewable developments, stating that the tax break shows favoritism to corporations over citizens who pay full property taxes. She expressed concern that these projects have no water-recycling capability and use significant amounts of water for both construction and ongoing maintenance. Ms. Quog stated her belief that the decisions made by the Board of Supervisors, the County Attorney, the County Manager, Real, AZ, Rose Law Firm, foreign corporations, and local property owners who have signed project leases demonstrate a lack of foresight regarding water conservation, environmental preservation, and community welfare. She noted that, in her view, these actions could lead to serious consequences for local communities. She further stated that members of the public are alarmed by what they perceive as private, closed-door meetings related to these projects. Ms. Quog expressed concern that, without effective water-management planning, the region could face significant water shortages. For these reasons, she requested that the Board impose an immediate moratorium on all utility-scale wind, solar, and battery energy storage project approvals until additional studies are completed. She urged the Board to consider the potential consequences of its decisions.

Casey Murph thanked the Board for the opportunity to speak, he stated that he is the 4th generation of his family to be born and 5th generation of his family to live in Perkins Valley, AZ. He stated that he is a cattle rancher that will be affected by the installation of a utility scale solar installation on state grazing lands. Mr. Murph said that he used his retirement to purchase his land from his relatives, pointing out that the price of beef has risen because in the past 40 years there have been 1 million independent cattle ranchers pushed out of business.  Mr. Murph said that utility solar uses land and pushes people like himself out of business.

Mr. Robert Black thanked the Board for the opportunity to speak.  He addressed the Board regarding renewable energy projects. He expressed disappointment with prior comments in which the Board stated that there was nothing that could be done if applicants met Planning and Zoning requirements. Mr. Black stated his belief that the Board does have options and presented a petition containing over 500 signatures opposing wind and solar projects, noting that signatures were collected within a week and continue to increase. Mr. Black reported concerns about an oily, sticky substance observed in cattle drinkers and on grasslands west of McLaws Road, which he believes to be associated with nearby renewable energy projects. He stated that he is having the substance tested and has retained a sample. He expressed concern that residents are being negatively affected and urged the Board not to dismiss public concerns. Mr. Black stated that public outcry is growing and indicated that residents will continue attending Board meetings until these issues are addressed. He expressed the view that it is the Board’s responsibility to protect citizens and work with the State if needed. He urged the Board to take action, stating that renewable projects are harming local communities and that residents are not willing to live with the current conditions. Mr. Black concluded by asking the Board to “figure it out,” reiterating that he believes it is the Board’s responsibility to do so.

WORK SESSION : Pursuant to ARS § 38-431.02 and ARS § 38-431(3), notice is hereby given to the members of the Navajo County Board of Supervisors and to the Public that a quorum of the Board of Supervisors may be present for a work session immediately following the Board of Supervisors Agenda in the Board Chambers where no official action will be taken.
10:24 a.m. Chairman Seymore called the Work Session to order.

a.

Presentation and review of Public Works projects, including the Capital Improvement Program and projects for the next fiscal year.

Bryan Layton talked about how the county has invested in upgrading its GIS mapping system, so residents can look up their streets, see who maintains them, and when maintenance occurred, with access via a QR code on business cards, which leads to the county website. He indicated that county roads are those not maintained by federal, state, municipal, or tribal entities, totaling about 300 miles of paved and 350 miles of unpaved roads. He indicated the new pavement management program, which replaced informal visual inspections with a complete, measured inventory that allows data-driven, resource-efficient decisions to keep the overall road network in the best possible condition.

Will Flake outlined the county’s approach to road preservation, keeping good roads in good condition, versus maintenance and rehabilitation, fixing more serious issues, covering both paved and gravel roads. He explains that a pavement condition survey conducted in August 2023 rated all paved sections with a Pavement Condition Index, showing an average Pavement Condition Index (PCI) of 73 with over 80% of roads in good or better condition, giving the county a clear baseline and roadmap for future work. He spoke about targeted pavement preservation and rehabilitation projects in specific areas, grouped to reduce cost and mobilization, including nearly two miles of rehabilitation on seven roads that currently have a low average PCI of 46, indicating they essentially need to be rebuilt once they reach that level of deterioration.

Vice-Chair Whitesinger
inquired if the scale was from 1 to 100.

Mr. Flake indicated that the PCI is scored on a 0–100 scale.  He explained that primitive gravel roads are graded at least every eight weeks, usually more often, with non‑primitive gravel roads on a four‑week cycle and high‑traffic routes targeted about every three weeks, all tracked on the county website by district and date. He provided a brief overview of the five‑year Capital Improvement Program covering engineering, flood control, highway maintenance, and fleet, updated annually to keep a rolling plan of funded projects.

Supervisor Benally asked about the difference between slurry seal, chip seal and resurfacing.

Mr. Flake explained that slurry seal is a thin oil-and-aggregate treatment used to fill small cracks and protect pavement from water damage, especially when it freezes in the winter, while chip seal is a similar preservation treatment usually applied after slurry seal, adding small rock chips to the oil layer to create a more durable protective surface.

Supervisor Benally inquired about the tar that was put into the cracks.

Mr. Flake indicated that it is called crack sealing, which is used as a preventative maintenance treatment, noting that while the county does not use it extensively, they do apply it in certain areas.

Chairman Seymore inquired about Borden Ranch Road, noting that a property owner granted right-of-way when the road was widened, with a promise that pavement would be extended to their properties and certain cattle guards installed.  He indicated that the that paving has not yet occurred.

Mr. Flake stated he would talk with staff regarding the concern.  Mr. Flake explained that CIP projects are reviewed annually, completed items are removed, and new needs added, with top priority given to public safety and preserving existing roads so they don’t deteriorate to the point of costly reconstruction, and he invited ongoing board input on priorities across their districts.

Chairman Seymore shared a concern regarding whether the county is allocating enough in capital improvements to at least maintain current overall road conditions in the face of inflation and long-term funding needs.

Mr. Flake indicated that they are working on gathering data and will provide more information.

Supervisor Peshlakai spoke about recent non-reservation projects in his district and asked how the county can partner with the City of Holbrook on improving the road to the Holbrook Golf Course in Perkins Valley.  He inquired how needs are identified with partner projects off reservation so he can be more engaged in those projects.

Mr. Flake indicated that the county has partnered with municipalities before through IGA's and financial contributions to city-led projects, confirming that such a partnership approach is definitely possible.

Rochelle Lacapa outlined the framework for partnering on non‑county transportation projects, which includes confirming the request fits within the board‑approved budget and $100,000 procurement threshold, giving priority to shovel‑ready projects with completed studies/designs and a clear scope and cost from the lead agency, and ensuring the work is realistically achievable within fiscal year procurement timelines.  She noted this framework is used consistently when working with ADOT, NDOT, White Mountain Apache, and municipalities on partnership projects.

Nicole Reske reported on District One and District Two partnerships, noting completed and in‑progress projects such as gravel improvements and pothole patching on key Navajo Nation routes, a legacy road project finished in April, and critical support for BIA by providing the only heavy equipment operator for the Defiance area, while outlining forecasted projects based on community and chapter priorities.

Mr. Flake highlighted partnership work in Districts 3 and 4, including ongoing Silver Creek flood-control cleaning with local partners, the planned Seventh Street extension in Snowflake, the future Scott Ranch Road infrastructure expansion, and early discussions with the City of Show Low about widening and reconstructing Ellsworth Road.

Mr. Layton explained that municipal partnership projects typically start when a city identifies a road with both city and county impacts, such as Ellsworth Road, approaches the county to explore a cost‑share, and then staff work through that process.  He encouraged the Supervisors to talk with city managers to identify such joint opportunities, noting he also meets regularly with city and county managers to discuss shared issues and potential projects.

Chairman Seymore spoke about a long‑standing regional project to complete a bridge and road connection near Home Depot, suggesting the county consider redirecting its Scott Ranch Road partnership funds to help the City of Show Low build the more attainable Wolfford Road bridge so at least one critical bridge project can move forward rather than waiting indefinitely on state or federal funding.

Mr. Flake indicated that the suggestion makes sense, noting the new bridge design is already being worked on.

Ms. Reske highlighted successful District 5 partnerships, including annual funding for much‑needed streetlights in Whiteriver and a bus turnaround improvement that now safely accommodates school buses, noting recent tribal requests for striping on Industrial Road and a new guardrail project on BIA 12.

Ms. Lacapa spoke about the guardrail project, noting it required complex coordination with both BIA and Gila County.  She used the BIA 12 guardrail project to illustrate how complex multi-jurisdictional partnerships can require coordination with another county’s management and legal teams to clarify roles and agreements, noting that working through this has created a template that will make similar cross‑county tribal projects easier going forward.

Ms. Reske explained that successful tribal partnerships depend on aligning priorities through regular meetings, using longer IGA's to fit tribal approval timelines, consistently educating communities about what county partnership funds can cover, and first confirming who owns each road and formally notifying that lead agency, especially on complex, cross‑jurisdictional reservation routes.

Ms. Lacapa commended Ms. Reske for significantly expanding and strengthening tribal transportation partnerships.  She likened coordinating with other agencies to timing your jump into a moving jump rope, where joining too early can disrupt their process, and said Nicole, Will, and Bryan have learned how to “jump in” at the right moment, so the county helps support, rather than slows, partner projects.

Mr. Layton praised Ms. Reske’s relationship‑building with tribal partners.  He asked if the Board had any feedback or direction on the CIP, data‑driven pavement management, or partnership efforts, emphasizing that while staff now knows exactly which roads are in the worst condition, there is still room for Board discretion based on what Supervisors see and hear in their districts.

Vice-Chair Whitesinger spoke about how far the county has come in organizing and prioritizing roadwork, praising the shift toward equity and fairness across districts rather than popularity or politics, and commended the professional, relationship‑based work with tribal communities, highlighting a recent direct outreach from a tribal department director as a sign that Navajo County is viewed as a reliable partner.

Chairman Seymore shared his appreciation for the collaborative work.

Mr. Layton provided a brief update on budget meetings with departments.
